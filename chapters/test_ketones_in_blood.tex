\chapter{Ketones (Blood)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
Blood ketone measurement quantifies beta-hydroxybutyrate (the predominant ketone body in diabetic ketoacidosis) or total ketones in serum or plasma. Ketones (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetone) are produced by hepatic beta-oxidation of fatty acids and serve as alternative fuel during glucose deprivation. Measurement is used to diagnose and manage diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), alcoholic ketoacidosis, and to monitor ketogenic diet therapy for epilepsy. Blood testing supersedes urine ketone detection because it measures beta-hydroxybutyrate directly, the principal ketone in DKA, and correlates better with clinical status \cite{dhatariya2020diabetic}.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Beta-Hydroxybutyrate
  \item BHB
  \item Blood Ketones
  \item Serum Ketones
  \item Plasma Ketones
  \item Acetoacetate
  \item Beta-Hydroxybutyric Acid
\end{itemize}
\section*{Principle}
Enzymatic method on automated chemistry analyzers using beta-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase: beta-hydroxybutyrate + NAD+ $\leftrightarrow$ acetoacetate + NADH + H+. The rate of NADH formation, measured at 340 nm, is proportional to beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration. POC meters (e.g., Abbott Precision Xtra, KetoScan) use similar enzyme-based test strips and provide results within 10 seconds from a fingerstick \cite{arora2011point}.

\section*{History}
Ketonuria was initially detected by Gerhardt's ferric chloride test (1865). Modern enzymatic quantification of beta-hydroxybutyrate was introduced in the 1970s. The shift from urine acetoacetate to blood beta-hydroxybutyrate reflected the understanding that in DKA beta-hydroxybutyrate predominates 3:1 over acetoacetate, and resolution of DKA recovery shifts acetoacetate back to BHB paradoxically increasing urine ketones.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Emergency evaluation of hyperglycemia with metabolic acidosis (suspected DKA)
  \item Diagnosis of alcoholic ketoacidosis in alcohol use disorder with vomiting and acidosis
  \item Monitoring treatment of DKA: aiming to halve beta-hydroxybutyrate every 2 hours with insulin
  \item Evaluation of hypoglycemia with ketosis (inborn errors of metabolism, starvation)
  \item Monitoring ketogenic diet therapy: target blood BHB 1.5--3.0 mmol/L for seizure control \cite{neal2008ketogenic}
  \item Differentiating DKA from hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
Primary test in suspected DKA and management of ketoacidosis.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
Venous blood drawn in serum or heparin tube; POC testing from fingerstick. As ketones are volatile and convert reversibly between BHB and acetoacetate, the sample is processed soon (lab results within 30--60 minutes; POC results within seconds).

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
None in emergency situations. For ketogenic diet monitoring, take fasting morning blood for consistency.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Urine ketone (nitroprusside) tests measure acetoacetate, not beta-hydroxybutyrate, and may paradoxically increase during DKA recovery despite clinical improvement. Capillary blood ketone meters and strips should be reasonably calibrated; falsely low readings occur with high acetone or prolonged storage. POC equipment is not as reliable as laboratory measurement in severe DKA.
\end{itemize}
\section*{Risks}
Fingerstick or venipuncture only.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
In suspected DKA, repeat beta-hydroxybutyrate every 2--4 hours with electrolytes, glucose, and ABG/venous blood gas. Treatment goal: BHB $<$ 1.5 mmol/L and anion gap closure.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
Low or normal ketones with ketosis signs may occur if acetoacetate predominates (alcoholic ketoacidosis, recovery from DKA); use clinical judgment.

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diabetic ketoacidosis:} BHB $>$ 3.0 mmol/L; insulin deficiency and counterregulatory hormone excess \cite{kitabchi2009hyperglycemic}
  \item \textbf{Alcoholic ketoacidosis:} BHB 2--10 mmol/L; dietary depletion of glycogen and alcohol metabolism
  \item \textbf{Starvation ketosis:} BHB 0.5--3.0 mmol/L; physiologic adaptation, usually benign
  \item \textbf{Ketogenic diet therapy:} 1.5--3.0 mmol/L targeted for seizure control
  \item \textbf{Inborn errors of metabolism:} beta-ketothiolase deficiency, carnitine palmitoyltransferase deficiencies
  \item \textbf{SGLT2 inhibitors (euglycemic DKA):} Normal or mildly elevated glucose with high BHB
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serum beta-hydroxybutyrate (fasting, normal):} 0.02--0.27 mmol/L
  \item \textbf{After overnight fast:} up to 0.6 mmol/L
  \item \textbf{Ketotic threshold:} 0.5--1.0 mmol/L (mild ketosis)
  \item \textbf{Ketoacidosis:} $>$ 3.0 mmol/L (probable); $>$ 4.8 mmol/L confirmatory
  \item \textbf{Anion gap:} $>$ 12 mEq/L with elevated BHB and metabolic acidosis is DKA
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Venous blood gas (VBG):} pH $<$ 7.30, bicarbonate $<$ 15 mEq/L confirms acidosis
  \item \textbf{Blood glucose:} $>$ 250 mg/dL typical in DKA; euglycemic DKA possible with SGLT2 inhibitors
  \item \textbf{Serum electrolytes and anion gap:} Anion gap $>$ 12 mEq/L
  \item \textbf{Serum beta-hydroxybutyrate (serial):} Every 2 hours during DKA management
  \item \textbf{Serum osmolality:} Differentiates DKA ($<$ 320 mOsm/kg) from HHS ($>$ 320 mOsm/kg)
  \item \textbf{Lactate, alcohol level, salicylate, acetaminophen:} Other causes of high-anion-gap acidosis
\end{itemize}
