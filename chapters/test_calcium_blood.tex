\chapter{Calcium (Blood)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
The calcium blood test measures the total concentration of calcium in the blood serum or plasma. Calcium is the most abundant mineral in the human body, with 99\% stored in bones and teeth as hydroxyapatite crystals [Ca\textsubscript{10}(PO\textsubscript{4})\textsubscript{6}(OH)\textsubscript{2}], and the remaining 1\% in extracellular fluid and soft tissues. In blood, calcium exists in three forms: (1) ionized calcium (free, physiologically active, approximately 50\% of total), (2) protein-bound calcium (approximately 40\%, primarily bound to albumin), and (3) calcium complexed with anions such as citrate, phosphate, and bicarbonate (approximately 10\%). The total calcium test measures all three fractions. Ionized calcium can be measured directly via ion-selective electrode. Calcium is critical for neuromuscular excitability (hypocalcemia increases sodium membrane permeability causing tetany; hypercalcemia decreases excitability), cardiac contractility (calcium influx through L-type channels initiates myocardial contraction), blood coagulation (Factor IV in the clotting cascade), hormone secretion, and bone mineralization. Serum calcium is tightly regulated by parathyroid hormone (PTH), 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), and calcitonin. PTH increases serum calcium by stimulating bone resorption, increasing renal calcium reabsorption, and stimulating renal conversion of 25-hydroxyvitamin D to active 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D. Because approximately 40\% of total calcium is albumin-bound, total calcium must always be corrected for albumin in patients with hypoalbuminemia: Corrected calcium (mg/dL) = measured total calcium + 0.8 $\times$ (4.0 -- albumin in g/dL).

\section*{Alternative Names}
Ca; Calcium, Serum; Total Serum Calcium; Ca2+; Ionized Calcium; Corrected Calcium; Albumin-Adjusted Calcium

\section*{Principle}
Total serum calcium is measured by spectrophotometric methods using colorimetric complex-forming reagents. The two most common methods are: (1) o-Cresolphthalein complexone (CPC) method: calcium ions react with o-cresolphthalein complexone in alkaline solution (pH 10--11) to form a violet-colored complex absorbing at 570--580 nm. Magnesium interference is prevented by adding 8-hydroxyquinoline. (2) Arsenazo III method: calcium reacts with the metallochromic dye arsenazo III to form a blue-colored complex measured at 650 nm; this method has higher sensitivity and less magnesium/phosphate interference. Ionized calcium is measured by ion-selective electrode (ISE) using a neutral carrier ionophore membrane. Ionized calcium measurement requires anaerobic sample handling because pH changes from CO\textsubscript{2} loss alter calcium-albumin binding. This test is performed on serum separator tubes using spectrophotometric methods, not by hematology analyzers.

\section*{History}
Calcium was isolated by Humphry Davy in 1808. The first colorimetric method for serum calcium was developed by Kramer and Tisdall in 1921 using calcium oxalate precipitation followed by permanganate titration. Clark and Collip refined this method in 1925. James Collip isolated parathyroid hormone in 1925. The o-cresolphthalein complexone method was introduced by Anderegg in 1954. The arsenazo III method followed in the 1970s. Elmer McCollum discovered vitamin D as the antirachitic factor in 1922, and Hector DeLuca characterized its metabolism to the active hormone calcitriol in the 1960s-1970s. The ionized calcium ISE was developed in the 1970s.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaluation of hypercalcemia symptoms: ``bones, stones, groans, psychiatric overtones''---bone pain, kidney stones, constipation/abdominal pain, confusion/depression/lethargy; ECG shows shortened QT interval; severe hypercalcemia (>14 mg/dL) risks cardiac arrest
  \item Evaluation of hypocalcemia symptoms: perioral/acral paresthesias, muscle cramps and tetany (carpopedal spasm), positive Chvostek sign and Trousseau sign, seizures, prolonged QT interval, laryngospasm
  \item Routine screening as part of the comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP)
  \item Diagnosis of primary hyperparathyroidism: most common cause of outpatient hypercalcemia---elevated calcium, low or inappropriately normal PTH, hypophosphatemia \cite{bilezikian2018}
  \item Monitoring of malignancy-related hypercalcemia: PTHrP secretion (lung, breast, renal, ovarian cancers), osteolytic metastases (breast, multiple myeloma), ectopic calcitriol (lymphomas)
  \item Monitoring of hypoparathyroidism: post-surgical (thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy), autoimmune, DiGeorge syndrome
  \item Assessment of renal failure: hyperphosphatemia in CKD complexes calcium; reduced calcitriol decreases intestinal absorption; secondary hyperparathyroidism develops
  \item Evaluation of pancreatitis: hypocalcemia is a Ranson criterion, caused by saponification of calcium with free fatty acids
  \item Monitoring patients on bisphosphonates, denosumab, thiazides, lithium, calcium/vitamin D supplements
  \item Screening for MEN syndromes: MEN1 (hyperparathyroidism, pituitary, pancreatic NET) and MEN2A (medullary thyroid, pheochromocytoma, hyperparathyroidism)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
Total serum calcium is a primary screening test as part of the CMP. An abnormal total calcium should always be followed by albumin correction and frequently by ionized calcium measurement, especially in critically ill patients.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
A healthcare professional draws blood from a vein into a serum separator tube (gold-top) or lithium heparin tube (green-top). Prolonged tourniquet use should be avoided---venous stasis causes local acidosis shifting calcium off albumin. The patient should be seated or supine for 5 minutes before draw since posture changes alter protein concentration, affecting total calcium by 0.2--0.4 mg/dL. The sample is centrifuged and analyzed on an automated chemistry analyzer. Results available in 30--60 minutes. For ionized calcium, whole blood is collected anaerobically in a heparinized syringe and analyzed within 15--30 minutes on a blood gas analyzer.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
No fasting is required for calcium alone; if part of CMP, 8-hour fast recommended. Avoid calcium or vitamin D supplements the morning of the test. Prolonged tourniquet time must be minimized. Discuss medications: thiazide diuretics (decrease urinary calcium excretion, may cause mild hypercalcemia), lithium (can cause hyperparathyroidism), bisphosphonates and denosumab (lower calcium), calcium and vitamin D supplements, and calcimimetics (cinacalcet). EDTA tubes (purple-top) must NOT be used---EDTA chelates calcium and causes falsely low results. Samples should be processed within 4 hours.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
No absolute contraindications. Critical pre-analytical considerations: (1) EDTA tube contamination---EDTA chelates calcium causing severe pseudohypocalcemia; always draw chemistry tubes before EDTA tubes. (2) Prolonged tourniquet time---venous stasis causes hemoconcentration, elevating total calcium by 0.2--0.5 mg/dL. (3) Posture: supine to standing causes 0.2--0.4 mg/dL increase. (4) Hypoalbuminemia: low albumin lowers total calcium proportionally---always calculate corrected calcium. Albumin correction formula: Corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.8 $\times$ (4.0 -- albumin). (5) Alkalosis (hyperventilation, bicarbonate infusion): increases calcium-albumin binding, reducing ionized calcium without changing total calcium---this is why tetany occurs with hyperventilation. (6) Acidosis: decreases calcium-albumin binding, increasing ionized calcium. (7) Citrate in transfused blood products chelates calcium, lowering ionized calcium. (8) Gadolinium contrast agents (MRI) can interfere with some colorimetric calcium methods for up to 24 hours. (9) Medications affecting results: thiazides (increase), loop diuretics (decrease), lithium (increase via PTH), bisphosphonates/denosumab (decrease).

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk. Mild pain or bruising at venipuncture site, resolving in 1--2 days. Rare: hematoma, infection, nerve injury, vasovagal syncope. Blood volume drawn (3--5 mL) is negligible.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results available in 30--60 minutes. Critical values: total calcium <6.5 mg/dL (risk of tetany, seizures, prolonged QT, cardiac arrest) or >14 mg/dL (risk of coma, cardiac arrest). Ionized calcium <3.0 mg/dL (0.75 mmol/L) or >6.0 mg/dL (1.5 mmol/L) are critical. Abnormal total calcium triggers: (1) albumin correction calculation, (2) assessment of PTH level (if elevated calcium with elevated/normal PTH $\rightarrow$ primary hyperparathyroidism; if elevated calcium with suppressed PTH $\rightarrow$ malignancy or other PTH-independent cause), (3) assessment of vitamin D metabolites, (4) evaluation for malignancy (PTHrP assay), and (5) ECG monitoring for QT changes.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypocalcemia (total Ca <8.5 mg/dL or ionized Ca <4.5 mg/dL):}
  \item \textbf{Hypoparathyroidism:} Post-surgical (thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy---most common cause; transient in 10--30\%, permanent in 1--3\%), autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1, DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), activating mutation of calcium-sensing receptor (autosomal dominant hypocalcemia), hypomagnesemia (magnesium <1.0 mg/dL impairs PTH secretion and end-organ response) \cite{cooper2008}
  \item \textbf{Vitamin D deficiency:} Inadequate intake/sun exposure, malabsorption (celiac disease, Crohn's, gastric bypass, short bowel), chronic kidney disease (decreased 1-alpha-hydroxylase), liver disease (decreased 25-hydroxylation), anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbital---induce vitamin D metabolism). 25-hydroxyvitamin D <20 ng/mL is deficient
  \item \textbf{Chronic kidney disease:} Hyperphosphatemia complexes calcium; decreased calcitriol production; secondary hyperparathyroidism with elevated PTH
  \item \textbf{Acute pancreatitis:} Saponification of calcium with free fatty acids in necrotic peripancreatic fat; hypocalcemia is a Ranson criterion for severe pancreatitis (Ca <8.0 mg/dL)
  \item \textbf{Hungry bone syndrome:} Rapid calcium uptake into bone after parathyroidectomy for severe hyperparathyroidism; profound, prolonged hypocalcemia requiring aggressive calcium and calcitriol replacement
  \item \textbf{Rhabdomyolysis:} Calcium deposition in damaged muscle; hyperphosphatemia from muscle breakdown complexes calcium
  \item \textbf{Tumor lysis syndrome:} Hyperphosphatemia leading to calcium-phosphate precipitation
  \item \textbf{Medications:} Bisphosphonates, denosumab, cinacalcet, loop diuretics (increase calciuria), calcitonin, foscarnet, citrate (massive transfusion), phenytoin
  \item \textbf{Critical illness:} Sepsis, burns, toxic shock syndrome---mechanisms include acquired hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, and calcium chelation
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypercalcemia (total Ca >10.5 mg/dL or ionized Ca >5.6 mg/dL):}
  \item \textbf{Primary hyperparathyroidism (most common outpatient cause):} Single parathyroid adenoma (85\%), hyperplasia (10--15\%), carcinoma (<1\%). Labs: elevated Ca, low or inappropriately normal PTH (PTH >20 pg/mL with hypercalcemia is abnormal), hypophosphatemia, hypercalciuria, normal or elevated 1,25(OH)\textsubscript{2}D. Associated with MEN1 and MEN2A
  \item \textbf{Malignancy (most common inpatient cause):} Humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy (PTHrP secretion by squamous cell lung, breast, renal, ovarian, head/neck cancers; PTHrP mimics PTH, labs show elevated Ca, suppressed PTH, elevated PTHrP); osteolytic metastases (breast, multiple myeloma---local bone destruction, labs show elevated Ca, suppressed PTH, normal/low PTHrP); ectopic calcitriol production (lymphomas, especially Hodgkin disease); and multiple myeloma (osteoclast activation from cytokines). Hypercalcemia in malignancy portends poor prognosis---median survival 1--3 months \cite{stewart2005}
  \item \textbf{Vitamin D toxicity:} Excessive supplementation (>10,000 IU/day prolonged); granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, berylliosis, fungal infections---activated macrophages express 1-alpha-hydroxylase, producing calcitriol autonomously from 25-OH vitamin D). Labs: elevated Ca, suppressed PTH, elevated 25(OH)D (supplements) or elevated 1,25(OH)\textsubscript{2}D (granulomatous disease)
  \item \textbf{Medications:} Thiazide diuretics (reduce calciuria---most common drug cause of mild hypercalcemia), lithium (stimulates PTH secretion in 10--15\% of patients), vitamin D/calcium supplements, vitamin A toxicity, theophylline, tamoxifen (flare hypercalcemia)
  \item \textbf{Milk-alkali syndrome:} Excessive calcium carbonate antacid ingestion (>2--3 g/day elemental calcium) combined with absorbable alkali; causes hypercalcemia, metabolic alkalosis, and acute kidney injury. Historically from milk and sodium bicarbonate (Sippy diet for peptic ulcer disease); now from calcium carbonate supplements for osteoporosis or dyspepsia
  \item \textbf{Hyperthyroidism:} Increased bone turnover from excess thyroid hormone; mild hypercalcemia in 10--20\%
  \item \textbf{Immobilization:} Prolonged bed rest, spinal cord injury, casting---increased bone resorption without mechanical loading; hypercalcemia can develop within 1--4 weeks of complete immobilization, especially in adolescents (high bone turnover) and patients with Paget disease
  \item \textbf{Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH):} Inactivating mutation of calcium-sensing receptor in parathyroid and renal tubule; lifelong mild hypercalcemia (10.5--12 mg/dL), normal or mildly elevated PTH, very low 24-hour urine calcium (<100 mg/24h), benign course; does not require parathyroidectomy. Calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio <0.01 distinguishes FHH from primary hyperparathyroidism
  \item \textbf{Tertiary hyperparathyroidism:} Longstanding secondary hyperparathyroidism (CKD, malabsorption) becomes autonomous; persistent hypercalcemia after correction of the underlying cause (e.g., after renal transplant)
  \item \textbf{Severity grading:} Mild 10.5--12 mg/dL, Moderate 12--14 mg/dL, Severe >14 mg/dL (medical emergency---coma, cardiac arrest risk)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Total calcium (adults):} 8.5--10.5 mg/dL (2.12--2.62 mmol/L)
  \item \textbf{Total calcium (children):} 8.8--10.8 mg/dL
  \item \textbf{Total calcium (newborns):} 7.6--10.4 mg/dL (lower in first 1--2 days of life)
  \item \textbf{Ionized calcium (adults):} 4.5--5.6 mg/dL (1.12--1.40 mmol/L)
  \item \textbf{Ionized calcium (children):} 4.8--5.5 mg/dL
  \item \textbf{Albumin-corrected calcium:} 8.5--10.5 mg/dL [Corrected = measured + 0.8 $\times$ (4.0 -- albumin)]
  \item \textbf{Critical low total Ca:} <6.5 mg/dL
  \item \textbf{Critical high total Ca:} >14 mg/dL
  \item \textbf{Conversion:} 1 mg/dL = 0.25 mmol/L; 1 mmol/L = 4.0 mg/dL
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Albumin level:} Required for corrected calcium calculation. Drawn from same sample.
  \item \textbf{Ionized calcium:} Definitively measures physiologically active calcium; essential in critically ill patients, during massive transfusion, in pancreatitis, and when total calcium is borderline.
  \item \textbf{Parathyroid hormone (PTH, intact):} Essential for differential diagnosis. Elevated Ca + elevated PTH $\rightarrow$ primary hyperparathyroidism. Elevated Ca + suppressed PTH $\rightarrow$ malignancy, vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali syndrome. Low Ca + low PTH $\rightarrow$ hypoparathyroidism. Low Ca + elevated PTH $\rightarrow$ vitamin D deficiency, CKD (secondary hyperparathyroidism).
  \item \textbf{Phosphorus/phosphate:} Low in primary hyperparathyroidism; elevated in CKD, hypoparathyroidism, tumor lysis.
  \item \textbf{Magnesium:} Hypomagnesemia causes functional hypoparathyroidism and end-organ PTH resistance; Mg <1.0 mg/dL impairs PTH secretion.
  \item \textbf{25-hydroxyvitamin D:} Assess vitamin D stores. Deficiency <20 ng/mL; insufficiency 20--30 ng/mL.
  \item \textbf{1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol):} Elevated in primary hyperparathyroidism, granulomatous disease, lymphomas; low in CKD, hypoparathyroidism.
  \item \textbf{PTH-related peptide (PTHrP):} If malignancy-associated hypercalcemia suspected with suppressed PTH.
  \item \textbf{24-hour urine calcium:} Distinguishes FHH (very low Ca excretion, <100 mg/24h, Ca/Cr clearance <0.01) from primary hyperparathyroidism (normal or elevated Ca excretion).
  \item \textbf{Serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP) and immunofixation:} For suspected multiple myeloma (hypercalcemia, anemia, renal failure, bone pain/lesions).
  \item \textbf{TSH:} Hyperthyroidism can cause mild hypercalcemia.
  \item \textbf{ECG:} QT interval assessment. Hypocalcemia prolongs QT; hypercalcemia shortens QT.
  \item \textbf{Renal ultrasound:} If nephrolithiasis is suspected from chronic hypercalcemia.
\end{itemize}
