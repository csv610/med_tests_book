\chapter{CA-125 (Cancer Antigen 125)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
CA-125 (cancer antigen 125) is a high-molecular-weight mucin glycoprotein (MUC16) that is expressed on the surface of cells derived from celomic epithelium (mesothelial cells of the pleura, pericardium, and peritoneum) and M\"ullerian duct derivatives (epithelial cells of the fallopian tubes, endometrium, and endocervix). It is the most widely used serum biomarker for epithelial ovarian cancer. CA-125 is detected by the monoclonal antibody OC125, developed by immunizing mice with an ovarian cancer cell line (OVCA 433). CA-125 is shed into the bloodstream and peritoneal fluid by ovarian cancer cells and by benign conditions that irritate the peritoneal or pleural surfaces. The test is most useful for monitoring response to treatment and detecting recurrence of epithelial ovarian cancer, rather than as a screening test. CA-125 is elevated in approximately 80--85\% of women with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer (stage III--IV) but in only 50\% of women with stage I disease, limiting its sensitivity for early detection.

\section*{Alternative Names}
CA-125; Cancer Antigen 125; CA 125; MUC16; Carbohydrate Antigen 125; Ovarian Cancer Antigen CA 125; Cancer-Associated Serum Antigen 125

\section*{Principle}
CA-125 is measured by a two-site sandwich chemiluminescent immunoassay (CLIA) or electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECLIA) on automated immunoassay analyzers. The assay uses two monoclonal antibodies (OC125 and M11) that recognize distinct epitopes on the CA-125/MUC16 molecule. Patient serum is incubated with a capture antibody and a labeled detection antibody. The resulting immune complex is captured on paramagnetic particles or coated wells, washed, and a chemiluminescent signal is generated. Signal intensity is proportional to the CA-125 concentration, calibrated against a reference standard. Results are reported in U/mL. Inter-assay variation between different manufacturers' CA-125 assays is significant due to differences in the epitopes recognized by monoclonal antibodies; serial CA-125 measurements should be performed using the same assay method. Commercial CA-125 assays are available on Roche Cobas, Abbott Architect, Siemens Atellica/ADVIA Centaur, and Beckman Coulter platforms.

\section*{History}
CA-125 was discovered in 1981 by Robert C. Bast Jr. and colleagues at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. After immunizing mice with the OVCA 433 ovarian cancer cell line and screening 1,250 hybridomas, the OC125 monoclonal antibody (clone 125) was identified as one that reacted specifically with ovarian cancer cells but not with normal ovarian tissue \cite{bast1981reactivity}. The target antigen, CA-125 (later cloned and identified as MUC16), was characterized in subsequent years. CA-125 was approved by the FDA in 1983 for monitoring ovarian cancer treatment response \cite{bast1983radioimmunoassay}. The CA-125-II assay (using the M11 antibody as the capture antibody alongside OC125 as the tracer) was introduced in the 1990s, providing improved precision. The landmark United Kingdom Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS), published in 2015--2021, evaluated multimodal screening (annual CA-125 with the Risk of Ovarian Cancer Algorithm [ROCA] followed by transvaginal ultrasound) in over 200,000 postmenopausal women and reported a modest 15--20\% reduction in ovarian cancer mortality that did not reach statistical significance in the primary analysis but showed a significant mortality reduction when prevalent cases were excluded \cite{jacobs2016ovarian}. Current guidelines recommend against CA-125 screening for ovarian cancer in average-risk women.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Monitoring treatment response in women with known epithelial ovarian cancer --- falling CA-125 levels indicate response to chemotherapy or surgery; rising levels indicate disease progression or recurrence. CA-125 measurement is a component of response criteria (GCIG CA-125 response criteria define a 50\% reduction maintained for 28 days)
  \item Detection of recurrence in women with treated ovarian cancer --- a rising CA-125 typically precedes clinical or radiographic evidence of recurrence by 3--6 months. The MRC OV05/EORTC 55955 trial demonstrated that early treatment of recurrence based on CA-125 alone (without symptoms or imaging findings) does not improve overall survival compared to waiting for clinical symptoms
  \item Evaluation of a pelvic mass in a postmenopausal woman --- CA-125 elevated >35 U/mL in a postmenopausal woman with a complex pelvic mass raises suspicion for epithelial ovarian cancer and warrants referral to a gynecologic oncologist. The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) combines CA-125, ultrasound findings, and menopausal status
  \item Risk stratification using OVA1 or ROMA (Risk of Ovarian Malignancy Algorithm): OVA1 combines CA-125, transferrin, transthyretin (prealbumin), apolipoprotein A1, and beta-2 microglobulin; ROMA combines CA-125 and HE4 (human epididymis protein 4) with menopausal status
  \item Diagnosis of peritoneal or pleural metastases from other malignancies (breast, lung, colon, pancreas) with elevated CA-125
  \item Monitoring of primary peritoneal carcinoma or fallopian tube carcinoma, which share the same CA-125 expression pattern as epithelial ovarian cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test}
CA-125 is a secondary test. It is NOT recommended as a screening test for ovarian cancer in average-risk women due to inadequate sensitivity for early-stage disease (50\% in Stage I) and poor specificity (elevated in numerous benign conditions). CA-125 is primarily used for monitoring confirmed ovarian cancer and as an adjunct in evaluating pelvic masses.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
For most patients, a health care professional will take a blood sample from a vein in your arm using a small needle. After the needle is inserted, a small amount of blood will be collected into a test tube or vial. You may feel a little sting when the needle goes in or out. This usually takes less than five minutes.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
No special preparation is required. Fasting is not necessary. CA-125 should NOT be drawn during menstruation (menstruation elevates CA-125), within 4 weeks of abdominal surgery or pelvic surgery, during active pelvic inflammatory disease, during peritonitis, or during pregnancy (first trimester CA-125 is often elevated). Serial CA-125 measurements should ideally be performed in the same laboratory using the same assay method. Patients should inform their healthcare provider about all abdominal or pelvic conditions that could elevate CA-125.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
No absolute contraindications. CA-125 has limited specificity --- it is an inflammation-sensitive marker that is elevated in a wide variety of benign gynecologic conditions: endometriosis, uterine leiomyomata (fibroids), menstruation, pregnancy (particularly first trimester), pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, ruptured ovarian cyst, and benign ovarian tumors. Non-gynecologic conditions that elevate CA-125: ascites (any cause --- cirrhosis, congestive heart failure, nephrotic syndrome), peritonitis (bacterial, tuberculous), pancreatitis, pericarditis, pleuritis, and recent abdominal or pelvic surgery. Malignancies other than ovarian cancer that elevate CA-125: primary peritoneal carcinoma, fallopian tube carcinoma, endometrial carcinoma, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (60--80\%), breast carcinoma, colorectal adenocarcinoma, and lung cancer. Specimen should be collected in a serum separator tube (SST, gold-top) or red-top tube. Approximately 10--15\% of women with epithelial ovarian cancer (particularly mucinous and clear cell histologies) have normal CA-125 levels.

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk. Slight pain or bruising at the venipuncture site. Rare: hematoma, infection, vasovagal syncope.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results are typically available within 1 to 2 hours. An elevated CA-125 level in a postmenopausal woman with a pelvic mass raises strong suspicion for ovarian cancer and warrants immediate referral to a gynecologic oncologist for definitive surgery (primary cytoreductive surgery with staging). An elevated CA-125 in a premenopausal woman with a pelvic mass is less specific given the higher prevalence of benign conditions (endometriosis, functional cysts), and further evaluation with pelvic ultrasound, CT, or MRI is indicated. For women with known ovarian cancer on treatment, falling CA-125 levels indicate response; rising levels after achieving a normal or nadir value suggest recurrence and warrant imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis).

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal (Normal Range)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item CA-125 <35 U/mL: Normal in >99\% of healthy postmenopausal women and >95\% of healthy premenopausal women. Does not exclude early-stage (stage I) ovarian cancer --- 50\% of women with stage I disease have a normal CA-125
  \item Normal CA-125 in a woman with documented ovarian cancer after primary treatment: Indicates complete clinical response to surgery and/or chemotherapy. Routine surveillance with CA-125 is recommended every 3 months for the first 2 years, every 6 months for years 3--4, and annually thereafter
  \item Normalization of elevated CA-125 after treatment: Indicates good response. A 50\% decrease in CA-125 that is maintained for $\geq$28 days defines a CA-125 response by GCIG criteria. A fall to the normal range maintained >1 month defines a complete CA-125 response
  \item Mucinous ovarian cancer and clear cell ovarian cancer: More frequently have normal or minimally elevated CA-125 even with advanced disease. Other tumor markers (CEA and CA 19-9 for mucinous tumors) may be more useful in these subtypes
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item CA-125 >35 U/mL in a postmenopausal woman with a complex adnexal mass: Approximately 75--80\% risk of malignancy; warrants immediate referral to gynecologic oncology. The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) formally quantifies this risk using CA-125 level, ultrasound score, and menopausal status. RMI >200 indicates high risk
  \item CA-125 >200 U/mL in a woman with a pelvic mass: Highly suspicious for ovarian cancer
  \item CA-125 >35 U/mL in a premenopausal woman: Can be due to endometriosis (the most common benign cause of CA-125 elevation in premenopausal women), uterine fibroids, menstruation (CA-125 levels are higher during menses), pelvic inflammatory disease, pregnancy (first trimester CA-125 rises from decidual tissue), ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, and ruptured ovarian cyst. Pre-menopausal CA-125 results must be interpreted cautiously
  \item Rising CA-125 in a woman with known ovarian cancer after achieving nadir: Defines biochemical recurrence. CA-125 doubling (from nadir or from the upper limit of normal) is associated with an 85--90\% probability of radiographically detectable recurrence within 3--6 months. The decision to initiate salvage chemotherapy based on CA-125 alone (without symptoms or imaging) is controversial --- the MRC OV05/EORTC 55955 trial showed no survival benefit from early treatment
  \item Elevation from non-ovarian conditions: Endometriosis (commonly CA-125 35--200 U/mL), cirrhosis with ascites (CA-125 may be markedly elevated >1,000 U/mL due to peritoneal irritation from ascites), congestive heart failure with pleural and peritoneal effusions, tuberculosis peritonitis, pancreatitis, recent abdominal surgery (transient elevation for 2--4 weeks), and any condition causing ascites or pleural effusion
  \item Elevation from non-ovarian malignancies: Pancreatic cancer, endometrial cancer, breast cancer, lung cancer, colorectal cancer, and primary peritoneal carcinoma (indistinguishable from ovarian cancer by CA-125 alone)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CA-125:} <35 U/mL (upper limit of normal for most laboratories and assays)
  \item \textbf{Premenopausal women:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Follicular phase: <35 U/mL
    \item During menstruation: May rise to 35--65 U/mL
    \item First trimester of pregnancy: May rise to 35--100 U/mL (due to CA-125 production by the decidua)
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Postmenopausal women:} <35 U/mL (elevation >35 U/mL in a postmenopausal woman is concerning and warrants investigation)
  \item \textbf{Age >50 years:} Upper limit may be set lower (e.g., <20 U/mL) by some laboratories, as CA-125 naturally increases slightly with age
  \item Approximately 1--2\% of healthy women have CA-125 >35 U/mL from benign causes; approximately 8--10\% of healthy women have CA-125 >35 U/mL during menstruation
  \item CA-125 results should always be interpreted in the context of menopausal status, menstrual cycle timing, and clinical/radiographic findings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal and transabdominal):} The gold standard imaging modality for evaluation of pelvic masses. Assesses cyst characteristics (simple, complex, solid, septated, papillary projections, ascites) to generate an ultrasound score and calculate the Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI)
  \item \textbf{HE4 (human epididymis protein 4):} A second-generation serum biomarker for epithelial ovarian cancer. HE4 is more specific than CA-125 because it is less commonly elevated in benign gynecologic conditions (endometriosis, fibroids). The ROMA (Risk of Ovarian Malignancy Algorithm) combines HE4, CA-125, and menopausal status to calculate the probability of epithelial ovarian cancer
  \item \textbf{OVA1 (multivariate index assay):} An FDA-cleared blood test that combines CA-125, transferrin, transthyretin (prealbumin), apolipoprotein A1, and beta-2 microglobulin to identify women with pelvic masses at high risk for malignancy who should be referred to a gynecologic oncologist
  \item \textbf{CT abdomen/pelvis with intravenous and oral contrast:} For further characterization of a pelvic mass, assessment for peritoneal carcinomatosis, omental caking, lymphadenopathy, and liver metastases. The primary staging modality for suspected ovarian cancer
  \item \textbf{Exploratory laparotomy with staging:} The gold standard for definitive diagnosis and treatment. Total abdominal hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TAH-BSO), omentectomy, peritoneal biopsies, pelvic and para-aortic lymph node dissection, and cytoreduction of all visible tumor (optimal cytoreduction = residual disease <1 cm)
  \item \textbf{Peritoneal fluid cytology (paracentesis):} If ascites is present, fluid analysis for malignant cells. Occult omental/peritoneal metastases may be detected even in the absence of an identifiable ovarian mass
  \item \textbf{CA 19-9 and CEA:} If a pelvic mass is suspected to represent a gastrointestinal primary (Krukenberg tumor) or mucinous ovarian tumor with GI origin
  \item \textbf{Genetic testing for BRCA1 and BRCA2:} All women with epithelial ovarian cancer (including fallopian tube and primary peritoneal carcinoma) should be offered genetic testing per NCCN guidelines. Approximately 15--20\% of epithelial ovarian cancers occur in women with germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations
\end{itemize}
