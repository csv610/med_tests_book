\chapter{Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
Auditory brainstem response testing measures electrical activity from the auditory nerve and brainstem after sound stimulation. It is an objective hearing test, especially useful when a person cannot reliably respond to behavioral hearing tests, including newborns and young children. It can help estimate hearing thresholds and evaluate the auditory pathway, but it is not a complete assessment of every aspect of hearing or language development.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item ABR
  \item Brainstem Auditory Evoked Response
  \item Auditory Evoked Potential
  \item BAER
  \item Newborn ABR Hearing Screen
\end{itemize}
\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
Small electrodes are placed on the scalp and earlobes or forehead, and soft earphones deliver clicks or tones. The equipment records waveforms generated by the auditory pathway while the patient remains still or asleep. Infants may be tested during natural sleep; sedation is occasionally considered by a specialist when movement prevents reliable recording.

\section*{Why It Is Ordered}
Newborn hearing screening; follow-up of a failed newborn screen; suspected hearing loss when behavioral testing is unreliable; assessment of auditory neuropathy; and selected evaluation of the auditory nerve or brainstem. Results may help determine whether a child needs diagnostic audiology, hearing aids, cochlear-implant evaluation, or other developmental support.

\section*{Preparation, Risks, and Limitations}
The ears should be free of excessive wax when possible, and the patient should be calm or asleep. The test is noninvasive and painless. Sedation, if used, carries medication-specific risks. A failed screening result does not by itself diagnose permanent hearing loss; middle-ear fluid, noise, movement, and technical factors can affect the recording, so diagnostic follow-up is essential.
