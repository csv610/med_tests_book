\chapter{Carbon Dioxide (CO\textsubscript{2})}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
The carbon dioxide blood test---properly called total carbon dioxide (TCO\textsubscript{2}) or serum bicarbonate (HCO\textsubscript{3}\textsuperscript{-}) on a chemistry panel---measures the total CO\textsubscript{2} content dissolved in serum or plasma. This measurement includes bicarbonate (HCO\textsubscript{3}\textsuperscript{-}, approximately 90--95\% of total), dissolved CO\textsubscript{2} gas (about 5\%), and carbonic acid (H\textsubscript{2}CO\textsubscript{3}, negligible at physiological pH). The test is essentially a measure of serum bicarbonate, which is the second most abundant extracellular anion after chloride, and is the body's primary buffer system. The carbonic acid--bicarbonate buffer system is governed by the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = 6.1 + log([HCO\textsubscript{3}\textsuperscript{-}] / [0.03 $\times$ pCO\textsubscript{2}]). Serum bicarbonate is regulated primarily by the kidneys through three processes: (1) reabsorption of virtually all filtered bicarbonate in the proximal tubule (85\%), (2) generation of new bicarbonate in the collecting duct via ammonium (NH\textsubscript{4}\textsuperscript{+}) excretion and titratable acid excretion, and (3) reclamation of bicarbonate in the distal nephron. The normal serum bicarbonate range is 22--28 mEq/L (mmol/L). TCO\textsubscript{2} is a standard component of the basic metabolic panel (BMP) and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP). It is critically important that TCO\textsubscript{2} on a chemistry panel differs from pCO\textsubscript{2} on an arterial blood gas (ABG); ABG pCO\textsubscript{2} measures the partial pressure of dissolved CO\textsubscript{2} gas in arterial blood (normal 35--45 mm Hg), while the chemistry panel measures total CO\textsubscript{2} content (mostly bicarbonate) in venous serum.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Total CO2; TCO2; Carbon Dioxide Content; Serum Bicarbonate; HCO3; Bicarbonate (HCO3-); CO2 on BMP/CMP; Total Carbon Dioxide; Venous CO2

\section*{Principle}
Total CO\textsubscript{2} on automated chemistry analyzers is measured by converting all CO\textsubscript{2} species to bicarbonate via alkalinization, then allowing bicarbonate to react in an enzymatic method. The most common method uses phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC): HCO\textsubscript{3}\textsuperscript{-} + phosphoenolpyruvate $\rightarrow$ oxaloacetate + phosphate. The oxaloacetate is then reduced to malate by malate dehydrogenase (MDH) with concomitant oxidation of NADH to NAD\textsuperscript{+}: oxaloacetate + NADH + H\textsuperscript{+} $\rightarrow$ malate + NAD\textsuperscript{+}. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm (as NADH is consumed) is directly proportional to the bicarbonate concentration. This enzymatic method is specific, precise, and has largely replaced older methods. Earlier methods included: (1) the Van Slyke manometric apparatus (measuring released CO\textsubscript{2} gas under vacuum after acidification of the sample), which was the gold standard from 1917 through the 1970s; (2) the Technicon AutoAnalyzer colorimetric method using phenolphthalein indicator; (3) ion-selective electrode (ISE) for dissolved CO\textsubscript{2} using a gas-permeable membrane with a pH electrode (Severinghaus PCO\textsubscript{2} electrode, used in blood gas analyzers). On blood gas analyzers, bicarbonate is calculated from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation using measured pH and pCO\textsubscript{2}, not directly measured. The chemistry panel TCO\textsubscript{2} value generally correlates well with calculated bicarbonate from ABG but is typically 1--2 mEq/L higher because the chemistry panel measures all CO\textsubscript{2} species including dissolved CO\textsubscript{2} and carbamino compounds. This test is performed on serum separator tubes using spectrophotometric enzymatic methods, not by hematology analyzers.

\section*{History}
The understanding of CO\textsubscript{2} transport in blood was pioneered by Christian Bohr (who discovered the Bohr effect) in the 1890s. Donald Van Slyke developed the manometric apparatus for measuring blood CO\textsubscript{2} content in 1917, which became the gold standard for 60 years and was central to the description of acid-base physiology. Van Slyke's work established the normal serum bicarbonate range of 22--28 mEq/L that is still used today. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation was formulated in 1908 by Lawrence J. Henderson (description) and 1917 by K.A. Hasselbalch (logarithmic transformation). The Severinghaus PCO\textsubscript{2} electrode was invented in 1958 by John Severinghaus, enabling rapid blood gas analysis. Enzymatic methods using PEPC were introduced in the 1970s-1980s and are now the standard for chemistry panels. The development of the basic metabolic panel (BMP) by automated chemistry analyzers in the 1970s made TCO\textsubscript{2} measurement a routine laboratory test.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Routine component of the basic metabolic panel (BMP) and comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP) for all patients; used to screen for acid-base disorders
  \item Evaluation of patients with suspected metabolic acidosis: hyperventilation (Kussmaul respirations), altered mental status, hypotension, abdominal pain (DKA), sepsis (lactic acidosis), aspirin overdose, ethylene glycol or methanol poisoning
  \item Evaluation of patients with suspected metabolic alkalosis: history of vomiting or NG suction, diuretic use, volume contraction (orthostasis, decreased skin turgor), hypertension and hypokalemia (hyperaldosteronism)
  \item Monitoring of chronic renal disease progression: metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate <22 mEq/L) is common when GFR falls below 20--30 mL/min and is associated with bone demineralization, muscle wasting, and accelerated CKD progression
  \item Perioperative monitoring: metabolic acidosis is common in critical illness and major surgery
  \item Assessment of compensation in respiratory disorders: chronic respiratory acidosis (COPD) increases renal bicarbonate reabsorption, elevating serum bicarbonate by 3.5 mEq/L for each 10 mm Hg rise in chronic pCO\textsubscript{2}; in chronic respiratory alkalosis (pregnancy, high altitude, hyperventilation), renal compensation decreases bicarbonate by 2--5 mEq/L for each 10 mm Hg drop in chronic pCO\textsubscript{2}
  \item Monitoring of patients on acetazolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) or topiramate, which cause metabolic acidosis
  \item Evaluation of unexplained hypokalemia: metabolic alkalosis causes intracellular potassium shift, worsening hypokalemia
  \item Part of the anion gap calculation: AG = Na -- (Cl + HCO\textsubscript{3}), essential for metabolic acidosis differential diagnosis \cite{kraut2007serum}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
TCO\textsubscript{2}/serum bicarbonate is a component of the basic metabolic panel, making it a primary screening test. It is always interpreted in the context of the complete acid-base profile, which in many cases requires arterial blood gas analysis for full characterization. Abnormal TCO\textsubscript{2} on a BMP is often the first clue to an underlying acid-base disorder and serves as a trigger for further investigation.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
A healthcare professional draws blood from a vein into a serum separator tube (gold-top or red-top). For most metabolic panels, a tourniquet is briefly applied and 3--5 mL of blood is collected. The tube must be filled completely---partially filled tubes leave headspace gas exchange that can alter CO\textsubscript{2} levels. The sample is centrifuged and tested on an automated chemistry analyzer using the PEPC enzymatic method. The tube cap should remain in place until analysis to minimize loss of CO\textsubscript{2} gas from the specimen. Results are available within 30--60 minutes. For a complete acid-base analysis including pCO\textsubscript{2} and pH, an arterial blood gas (ABG) must be obtained separately from the radial or femoral artery; this is a different test conducted on a blood gas analyzer, and should not be confused with the venous TCO\textsubscript{2} on the chemistry panel.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
No special preparation or fasting is required for serum TCO\textsubscript{2} measurement. However, certain factors can affect results if not controlled: (1) The blood tube must be filled completely and kept capped until centrifugation to avoid CO\textsubscript{2} off-gassing, which would artificially lower TCO\textsubscript{2}. (2) Prolonged tourniquet application during phlebotomy causes localized stasis and CO\textsubscript{2} accumulation from tissue metabolism, which can slightly elevate TCO\textsubscript{2}. (3) Recent ingestion of large amounts of antacids (sodium bicarbonate, calcium carbonate) or citrate (in blood transfusions) can elevate bicarbonate. (4) Vigorous exercise immediately before phlebotomy produces lactic acid, transiently lowering bicarbonate. (5) inform your provider of all medications: diuretics (both loop and thiazide can cause metabolic alkalosis), acetazolamide (metabolic acidosis), topiramate (metabolic acidosis), potassium citrate (urinary alkalinizer), sodium bicarbonate therapy, and corticosteroids (can cause metabolic alkalosis). (6) Samples should be processed within 4 hours of collection.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
No absolute contraindications for venipuncture. Important pre-analytical and analytical considerations: (1) Uncapped or partially filled tubes: CO\textsubscript{2} gas escapes from the sample into room air, lowering measured TCO\textsubscript{2}---this is the most common pre-analytical cause of spuriously low bicarbonate. Tubes should remain capped at all times. (2) Air bubbles in the sample: can promote CO\textsubscript{2} gas exchange and lower measured TCO\textsubscript{2}. (3) Delayed processing without refrigeration: glycolysis continues in red blood cells, generating lactate and CO\textsubscript{2}, and the latter may be lost to the air; this can unpredictably alter results. (4) Acetazolamide, topiramate, zonisamide: carbonic anhydrase inhibitors that predictably lower serum bicarbonate by 3--5 mEq/L. (5) Sodium bicarbonate administration: directly elevates measured TCO\textsubscript{2}. (6) Pregnancy: physiological chronic respiratory alkalosis (increased minute ventilation from progesterone) causes compensatory decrease in serum bicarbonate to approximately 18--22 mEq/L. This is normal in pregnancy and if a pregnant patient has a bicarbonate of 26 mEq/L, this may represent a superimposed metabolic alkalosis. (7) High altitude: chronic respiratory alkalosis with compensatory metabolic acidosis lowers bicarbonate. (8) Contamination with high-lactate fluid from a nearby IV site. (9) pH and temperature: the enzymatic PEPC method operates optimally at pH 7.8--8.0; extreme acidosis or alkalosis in the sample has negligible effect.

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk from venipuncture. Mild pain, bruising, or hematoma at the venipuncture site are the most common adverse events and self-resolve within 1--2 days. Rare complications: infection, nerve injury, excessive bleeding in patients with coagulopathy, and vasovagal syncope. No special risks beyond standard venipuncture. The blood volume drawn (3--5 mL) is negligible.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
A bandage is applied and can be removed after 15--30 minutes. Results are typically available in 30--60 minutes. TCO\textsubscript{2} (bicarbonate) is never interpreted alone; it is evaluated with sodium, potassium, chloride for anion gap calculation, and with the complete clinical picture. Key interpretation steps: (1) Calculate the anion gap [Na -- (Cl + HCO\textsubscript{3})] to determine if a metabolic acidosis is present and whether it is a high-AG or normal-AG (hyperchloremic) acidosis. (2) Assess whether the change in bicarbonate is primary (metabolic disorder) or compensatory (respiratory disorder) \cite{seifter2014integration}. (3) If an unexpected acid-base disorder is found, obtain ABG for pH and pCO\textsubscript{2}, and consider serum lactate, ketones, toxic alcohol screen, and osmolal gap. Critical values: bicarbonate <10 mEq/L or >40 mEq/L represents severe acid-base disturbance requiring immediate investigation and management. In DKA, bicarbonate <10 mEq/L indicates severe acidosis. In severe metabolic alkalosis, bicarbonate >40 mEq/L with pH >7.55 carries risk of cardiac arrhythmia.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Low serum bicarbonate (HCO\textsubscript{3} <22 mEq/L):} Indicates metabolic acidosis with or without compensatory respiratory alkalosis. Must be interpreted with the anion gap.
  \item \textbf{High-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (AG >12 mEq/L):} Mnemonic MUDPILES: Methanol (formic acid), Uremia (advanced renal failure, GFR <15, inability to excrete sulfate, phosphate, organic acid anions), Diabetic ketoacidosis (beta-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate), Propylene glycol / Paraldehyde / Pyroglutamic acid (acetaminophen-associated), Isoniazid or Iron overdose, Lactic acidosis (Type A: tissue hypoxia---shock, cardiac arrest, severe anemia, mesenteric ischemia, carbon monoxide poisoning; Type B: impaired cellular metabolism---sepsis, malignancy, metformin, liver failure, mitochondrial disorders, thiamine deficiency, D-lactate from short bowel syndrome), Ethylene glycol (glycolic and oxalic acid), Salicylates (aspirin causes mixed high-AG metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis). Lactic acidosis is the most common cause of elevated anion gap. Anion gap >20 mEq/L strongly suggests toxic alcohol ingestion, DKA, or severe lactic acidosis. Serum osmolal gap >10 mOsm/kg suggests methanol or ethylene glycol.
  \item \textbf{Normal-anion-gap (hyperchloremic) metabolic acidosis (AG 8--12, chloride elevated):} (a) Gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss: diarrhea (severe), pancreatic or biliary fistula, urinary diversion (ureterosigmoidostomy---chloride-bicarbonate exchange in colon); (b) Renal tubular acidosis---Type I (distal RTA: inability to secrete H+, urine pH >5.5 despite acidosis, causes include Sj\"{o}gren's, amphotericin B, lithium); Type II (proximal RTA: impaired HCO\textsubscript{3} reabsorption, Fanconi syndrome, multiple myeloma, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors); Type IV (hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism: diabetes, NSAIDs, ACE-I, trimethoprim---most common RTA); (c) Large volume normal saline infusion (hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis from supraphysiologic chloride load of 154 mEq/L); (d) Parenteral nutrition with excess chloride; (e) Ammonium chloride or arginine HCl ingestion.
  \item \textbf{Chronic respiratory alkalosis (compensatory):} Pregnancy (bicarbonate 18--22 mEq/L), high altitude, chronic hyperventilation, liver disease, salicylate intoxication (early phase), anxiety/hyperventilation syndrome, pulmonary embolism, congestive heart failure.
  \item \textbf{Severe bicarbonate <15 mEq/L:} Significant metabolic acidosis. <10 mEq/L: severe, often life-threatening acidosis. <5 mEq/L: extreme acidosis, usually incompatible with life without aggressive intervention.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{High serum bicarbonate (HCO\textsubscript{3} >28 mEq/L):} Indicates metabolic alkalosis (or compensatory response to chronic respiratory acidosis). Metabolic alkalosis is the most common acid-base disorder in hospitalized patients.
  \item \textbf{Chloride-responsive metabolic alkalosis (urine Cl <10--20 mEq/L):} Most common form; responds to volume and chloride repletion with normal saline. Causes: (a) GI loss of HCl: vomiting, nasogastric suction (gastric fluid contains 120--160 mEq/L HCl), congenital chloridorrhea; (b) Renal loss: diuretic therapy (loop and thiazide), post-hypercapnic state (rapid correction of chronic hypercapnia with ventilator leaves behind elevated bicarbonate from renal compensation), cystic fibrosis (excessive sweat chloride loss); (c) Villous adenoma of colon (rare, secretes chloride-rich fluid); (d) Recovery from starvation with carbohydrate refeeding. The hallmark is that urine chloride is low because the kidney is avidly reabsorbing chloride to correct the volume deficit.
  \item \textbf{Chloride-resistant metabolic alkalosis (urine Cl >20 mEq/L):} Requires treatment of underlying cause, not just volume/chloride. Causes: (a) Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome: hypokalemia, hypertension, metabolic alkalosis, suppressed renin, elevated aldosterone); (b) Secondary hyperaldosteronism (renal artery stenosis, renin-secreting tumor, heart failure, cirrhosis); (c) Cushing's syndrome (excess cortisol activates mineralocorticoid receptor); (d) Apparent mineralocorticoid excess (licorice ingestion containing glycyrrhizinic acid, carbenoxolone); (e) Liddle syndrome (constitutive activation of ENaC sodium channel); (f) Bartter syndrome (thick ascending limb defect resembling furosemide effect) and Gitelman syndrome (distal tubule defect resembling thiazide effect); (g) Severe hypokalemia (K <2.5 mEq/L stimulates ammoniagenesis and bicarbonate generation); (h) Excessive alkali administration (milk-alkali syndrome---calcium carbonate antacids plus calcium-containing foods; sodium bicarbonate therapy).
  \item \textbf{Chronic respiratory acidosis (compensatory):} COPD, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, neuromuscular disorders (ALS, muscular dystrophy), severe kyphoscoliosis, opiate overdose with chronic hypoventilation. In chronic respiratory acidosis, renal compensation increases serum bicarbonate by approximately 3.5 mEq/L for every 10 mm Hg rise in chronic pCO\textsubscript{2} above 40 mm Hg, with a maximum bicarbonate of ~45 mEq/L. Beyond this, a primary metabolic alkalosis is likely superimposed.
  \item \textbf{Bicarbonate >40 mEq/L:} Severe metabolic alkalosis. Associated with increased mortality in ICU patients. pH >7.55 carries risk of cardiac arrhythmias, decreased cerebral blood flow, reduced ionized calcium, and compensatory hypoventilation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adults:} 22--28 mEq/L (mmol/L)
  \item \textbf{Children (1--16 years):} 20--28 mEq/L
  \item \textbf{Infants (1--12 months):} 18--26 mEq/L
  \item \textbf{Newborns (0--7 days):} 17--25 mEq/L (lower due to immature renal acid excretion)
  \item \textbf{Pregnancy (third trimester):} 18--22 mEq/L (physiologic respiratory alkalosis with compensatory metabolic acidosis)
  \item \textbf{Critical low value:} <10 mEq/L
  \item \textbf{Critical high value:} >40 mEq/L
  \item \textbf{Normal anion gap:} 8--12 mEq/L [Calculated as Na -- (Cl + HCO\textsubscript{3})]
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG):} Provides pH, pCO\textsubscript{2}, pO\textsubscript{2}, and calculated bicarbonate. Essential for complete acid-base characterization. Allows differentiation between primary metabolic disorder and respiratory compensation. Also allows assessment of the adequacy of compensation using Winter's formula (expected pCO\textsubscript{2} = 1.5 $\times$ HCO\textsubscript{3} + 8 $\pm$ 2 for metabolic acidosis; expected increase in HCO\textsubscript{3} = 0.35 $\times \Delta$pCO\textsubscript{2} for acute respiratory acidosis, 0.35--0.55 for chronic).
  \item \textbf{Anion gap calculation:} AG = Na -- (Cl + HCO\textsubscript{3}). Used to differentiate high-AG from normal-AG metabolic acidosis. If AG is elevated, calculate delta-delta: (measured AG -- 12) + measured HCO\textsubscript{3}. If sum is >28, concurrent metabolic alkalosis is present. If <22, concurrent normal-AG metabolic acidosis is present.
  \item \textbf{Serum lactate:} Most common cause of elevated anion gap metabolic acidosis. Normal <2 mmol/L. >4 mmol/L is associated with significantly increased mortality in sepsis. Point-of-care lactate is standard in emergency departments.
  \item \textbf{Serum ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate):} For suspected DKA or alcoholic ketoacidosis. Beta-hydroxybutyrate is the predominant ketone in DKA. Urine ketone sticks do not detect beta-hydroxybutyrate and should not be used to monitor DKA treatment.
  \item \textbf{Serum osmolality and osmolal gap:} Measured osmolality -- Calculated osmolality [2(Na) + Glucose/18 + BUN/2.8 + Ethanol/4.6]. Osmolal gap >10 mOsm/kg suggests toxic alcohol (methanol, ethylene glycol, isopropanol). Calculate in unexplained high-AG metabolic acidosis.
  \item \textbf{Serum salicylate level:} If salicylate toxicity suspected (tinnitus, hyperventilation, mixed high-AG metabolic acidosis with respiratory alkalosis in adults).
  \item \textbf{Toxic alcohol screen (methanol, ethylene glycol):} If high-AG metabolic acidosis with elevated osmolal gap, plus appropriate exposure history (suicidal ingestion, industrial exposure, antifreeze).
  \item \textbf{Urine chloride:} Distinguishes chloride-responsive (urine Cl <10--20 mEq/L) from chloride-resistant (urine Cl >20 mEq/L) metabolic alkalosis.
  \item \textbf{Plasma renin activity and aldosterone:} When hyperaldosteronism is suspected as cause of metabolic alkalosis with hypertension and hypokalemia.
  \item \textbf{Comprehensive metabolic panel with BUN and creatinine:} To assess renal function as cause or consequence of acid-base disorder.
\end{itemize}
