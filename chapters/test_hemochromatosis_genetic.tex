\chapter{Hereditary Hemochromatosis Genetic Test}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
Hereditary-hemochromatosis genetic testing detects pathogenic variants in the HFE gene, most commonly C282Y and H63D. HFE-related hemochromatosis can cause excessive intestinal iron absorption and progressive iron deposition in the liver, pancreas, heart, joints, and other tissues.

\section*{Alternative Names}
HFE Gene Test; Hemochromatosis Mutation Test; C282Y and H63D Test; Hereditary Iron Overload Genetic Test

\section*{How It Is Performed}
DNA is extracted from blood or another validated specimen and analyzed for common HFE variants using PCR-based genotyping or sequencing. Iron studies, especially transferrin saturation and ferritin, are interpreted alongside the genetic result.

\section*{Why It Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaluation of persistently elevated transferrin saturation or ferritin when iron overload is suspected
  \item Investigation of unexplained liver disease, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, arthropathy, or bronze skin pigmentation with compatible iron studies
  \item Testing adult first-degree relatives of a person with confirmed HFE-related hemochromatosis
  \item Clarification of a suspected inherited cause of iron overload
\end{itemize}

\section*{Interpretation and Limitations}
A C282Y homozygous result strongly supports HFE-related susceptibility, but clinical iron overload is variable and cannot be predicted from genotype alone. C282Y/H63D compound heterozygosity and isolated H63D generally confer lower risk. A negative common-variant test does not exclude non-HFE hemochromatosis or other causes of high ferritin. Genetic results should be interpreted with iron studies, liver assessment, family history, and clinical findings.
\section*{Specimen and Preparation}
Testing usually uses EDTA blood and does not require fasting. Transferrin saturation and ferritin are often measured at the same evaluation and may have separate collection instructions. Provide information about alcohol use, inflammation, liver disease, transfusions, iron supplements, and affected relatives because these can alter iron studies without changing the genotype.

\section*{Risks and Safety}
Physical risk is limited to blood collection. A genotype is not a measure of current iron burden and should not be used alone to diagnose or exclude organ injury. Genetic counseling is appropriate when results may affect relatives or future health planning.

\section*{Understanding Results and Follow-up}
A positive HFE result is followed by iron studies and assessment for organ involvement when indicated. A negative common-variant panel does not rule out non-HFE hemochromatosis, chronic liver disease, inflammation, transfusion-related iron overload, or other causes of high ferritin. First-degree relatives may be offered targeted testing after counseling. Treatment decisions depend on biochemical iron overload and clinical findings, not genotype alone.

\section*{Regulatory Status and Authorization Date}
Regulatory status applies to the specific assay, instrument, manufacturer, and intended use rather than to the test name alone. No single approval date can be assigned to this test category. For a commercial assay, verify the current product in the relevant regulator's database: the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance, approval, or authorization; India's Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) licence or permission; the European Union In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (EU IVDR) CE certificate issued through the applicable conformity-assessment route; or the United Kingdom Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) registration and UKCA/CE status. Laboratory-developed tests may not have an individual FDA, CDSCO, EU IVDR, or MHRA approval date.
