\chapter{ANCA (Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
The antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) test detects autoantibodies directed against enzymes in the cytoplasmic granules of neutrophils and monocytes. ANCA testing is essential for the diagnosis and monitoring of ANCA-associated vasculitides (AAV), a group of rare, life-threatening autoimmune diseases characterized by necrotizing inflammation of small blood vessels. The two major target antigens are proteinase-3 (PR3) and myeloperoxidase (MPO). Testing is performed by two complementary methods: indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) on ethanol-fixed human neutrophils, which identifies the pattern (cytoplasmic/c-ANCA or perinuclear/p-ANCA), and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for antigen-specific antibodies to PR3 and MPO. The combination of IIF and ELISA provides optimal sensitivity and specificity. ANCA-associated vasculitides include granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener granulomatosis), microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA, formerly Churg-Strauss syndrome).

\section*{Alternative Names}
ANCA; Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies; c-ANCA (Cytoplasmic ANCA); p-ANCA (Perinuclear ANCA); Anti-PR3 (Anti-Proteinase-3); Anti-MPO (Anti-Myeloperoxidase); Vasculitis Antibody Panel

\section*{Principle}
ANCA testing is performed by two complementary methods. Indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) uses ethanol-fixed human neutrophils as substrate. Patient serum is incubated with neutrophil substrate, and fluorescein-conjugated anti-human IgG is added. Ethanol fixation disrupts cytoplasmic granules: positively charged MPO diffuses to and redistributes around the nucleus (producing perinuclear staining, p-ANCA), while PR3 remains in the cytoplasm (producing cytoplasmic staining, c-ANCA). Formalin-fixed neutrophils are used as a confirmatory substrate --- on formalin, MPO does not redistribute and produces cytoplasmic staining, while PR3 continues to show cytoplasmic staining. IIF results are reported as pattern (c-ANCA or p-ANCA) and titer. ELISA testing for PR3-specific and MPO-specific antibodies uses purified native or recombinant PR3 and MPO antigens coated onto microtiter plates. Signal detection uses enzyme-conjugated anti-human IgG with chromogenic or chemiluminescent detection. The international consensus (1999 and 2017) recommends that ANCA should be screened by both IIF and antigen-specific ELISA to maximize diagnostic accuracy \cite{savige1999international,bossuyt2017revised}.

\section*{History}
The association between ANCA and vasculitis was first reported in 1982 by David Davies and colleagues in Melbourne \cite{davies1982segmental}, who described segmental necrotizing glomerulonephritis in patients with antibodies to neutrophil cytoplasm. In 1985, Fokko van der Woude and colleagues in the Netherlands demonstrated the strong association between c-ANCA and Wegener granulomatosis (now GPA) \cite{vanderwoude1985autoantibodies}. The target antigens were subsequently identified: PR3 in 1988 and MPO in 1988--1989. The 1994 Chapel Hill Consensus Conference established standardized definitions for vasculitis, and the 1999 International Consensus Statement on ANCA Testing (Savige et al.) established the recommendation for dual testing by IIF and ELISA \cite{savige1999international}. The 2017 revised consensus statement reaffirmed this approach \cite{bossuyt2017revised}. The nomenclature was updated in 2012 from ``Wegener granulomatosis'' to ``granulomatosis with polyangiitis'' and from ``Churg-Strauss syndrome'' to ``eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis'' \cite{jennette2013revised}. Recent clinical trials (RAVE, RITUXVAS) have established rituximab as non-inferior to cyclophosphamide for remission induction in ANCA-associated vasculitis \cite{stone2010rituximab}.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Diagnosis of granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) in patients with upper and lower respiratory tract involvement, pulmonary nodules or cavities, and necrotizing glomerulonephritis. c-ANCA with anti-PR3 specificity is found in approximately 90\% of generalized GPA
  \item Diagnosis of microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) in patients with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, pulmonary hemorrhage (pulmonary-renal syndrome), and necrotizing vasculitis of small vessels. p-ANCA with anti-MPO specificity is found in 50--75\% of MPA
  \item Evaluation of rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN) --- ANCA-positive pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis is the most common cause of RPGN in older adults
  \item Investigation of pulmonary-renal syndrome (diffuse alveolar hemorrhage with acute renal failure)
  \item Diagnosis of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) --- ANCA is positive in 30--50\% of patients, typically p-ANCA/anti-MPO
  \item Screening for ANCA-associated vasculitis in patients with unexplained multisystem disease involving the kidneys, lungs, skin, and peripheral nerves
  \item Monitoring of disease activity in known ANCA-associated vasculitis --- rising ANCA titers may herald a relapse, though treatment decisions should not be based on ANCA titers alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Is This a Primary or Secondary Test}
This is a primary diagnostic test for ANCA-associated vasculitides. When clinical suspicion is moderate to high (e.g., rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, pulmonary hemorrhage, mononeuritis multiplex), ANCA testing is a first-line investigation. A positive ANCA result often obviates the need for invasive procedures but should be confirmed by kidney or lung biopsy in most cases.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
For most patients, a health care professional will take a blood sample from a vein in your arm using a small needle. After the needle is inserted, a small amount of blood will be collected into a test tube or vial. You may feel a little sting when the needle goes in or out. This usually takes less than five minutes.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
No special preparation is required. Fasting is not necessary. Patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy (cyclophosphamide, rituximab, corticosteroids) should inform their healthcare provider, as treatment may lower ANCA titers.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
No absolute contraindications. Specimen should be collected in a serum separator tube (SST, gold-top) or red-top tube. Hemolyzed, lipemic, or icteric specimens may interfere with immunofluorescence interpretation. ANCA positivity by ELISA or IIF should always be confirmed by the complementary method. Atypical ANCA patterns (atypical p-ANCA) that are negative for both anti-PR3 and anti-MPO by ELISA are commonly seen in non-vasculitic conditions: inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis, 60--80\%; Crohn disease, 10--30\%), autoimmune hepatitis (type 1), primary sclerosing cholangitis, and rheumatoid arthritis. These atypical ANCA target antigens other than PR3/MPO (lactoferrin, elastase, cathepsin G, BPI) and are not associated with vasculitis. ANCA can be falsely positive in patients with hydralazine-induced vasculitis (anti-MPO), propylthiouracil-associated vasculitis, and levamisole-adulterated cocaine use.

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk. Slight pain or bruising at the venipuncture site. Rare: hematoma, infection, vasovagal syncope.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results are typically available within 2 to 5 days. A positive c-ANCA with anti-PR3 specificity in a patient with appropriate clinical features strongly suggests GPA. A positive p-ANCA with anti-MPO specificity supports a diagnosis of MPA or EGPA. Tissue biopsy (kidney, lung, skin, nerve, or nasal/sinus) demonstrating pauci-immune necrotizing vasculitis or crescentic glomerulonephritis remains the gold standard for confirming the diagnosis. Serial ANCA titers may be monitored during treatment and remission, though treatment decisions should not be based solely on ANCA trends.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal (Negative)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A negative ANCA does not exclude ANCA-associated vasculitis --- 10--15\% of patients with biopsy-proven GPA are ANCA-negative, particularly those with limited (localized) disease confined to the upper respiratory tract
  \item Approximately 15--20\% of patients with microscopic polyangiitis are ANCA-negative on both IIF and ELISA
  \item Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is ANCA-negative in 50--70\% of patients, particularly those with predominantly eosinophilic manifestations (asthma, eosinophilia, peripheral neuropathy) rather than renal disease
  \item Anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) disease (Goodpasture syndrome) is characteristically ANCA-negative, though dual positivity (anti-GBM and ANCA) occurs in 20--30\% of cases
  \item Other causes of pulmonary-renal syndrome (SLE, IgA vasculitis, cryoglobulinemia) are typically ANCA-negative
  \item Immunosuppressive therapy can cause false-negative ANCA results
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal (Positive)}
\begin{itemize}
  \item c-ANCA with anti-PR3 specificity: Highly sensitive (85--90\%) and specific for granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA). A positive c-ANCA in the setting of active generalized GPA (with renal involvement) has a sensitivity approaching 95\%. Anti-PR3 antibodies are associated with a higher rate of relapse after remission
  \item p-ANCA with anti-MPO specificity: Found in 50--75\% of microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) and 30--50\% of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA). Anti-MPO antibodies are associated with renal-limited disease and pauci-immune crescentic glomerulonephritis. Anti-MPO-positive vasculitis has a lower relapse rate than anti-PR3-positive disease
  \item p-ANCA (atypical) without anti-PR3 or anti-MPO specificity by ELISA: Strongly associated with inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis 60--80\%, Crohn disease 10--30\%), autoimmune hepatitis type 1, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), and rheumatoid arthritis. These atypical p-ANCA target antigens include lactoferrin, elastase, cathepsin G, and bactericidal/permeability-increasing protein (BPI). This pattern is NOT associated with vasculitis and should not lead to unnecessary investigations or treatment for AAV
  \item Drug-induced ANCA-positive vasculitis: Hydralazine, propylthiouracil, minocycline, allopurinol, and penicillamine can induce anti-MPO ANCA with clinical vasculitis (skin ulcers, glomerulonephritis, pulmonary hemorrhage). Discontinuation of the offending drug typically leads to resolution
  \item Cocaine-levamisole syndrome: Levamisole-adulterated cocaine produces ANCA (often both anti-PR3 and anti-MPO) associated with retiform purpura, necrotic skin lesions, leukopenia, and pauci-immune glomerulonephritis
  \item Rising ANCA titer during follow-up: A four-fold rise in ANCA titer is associated with a 40--60\% risk of relapse within the subsequent 12 months, but serial ANCA monitoring alone should not dictate treatment changes in the absence of clinical manifestations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ANCA by indirect immunofluorescence (IIF):} Negative (no cytoplasmic or perinuclear staining at a 1:20 dilution)
  \item \textbf{Anti-PR3 (anti-proteinase-3) ELISA:} <20 U/mL (negative)
  \item \textbf{Anti-MPO (anti-myeloperoxidase) ELISA:} <20 U/mL (negative)
  \item \textbf{Healthy population:} Anti-PR3 and anti-MPO antibodies are extremely rare in healthy individuals (<1\%)
  \item \textbf{False-positive ANCA by IIF:} Low-titer p-ANCA without ELISA specificity is common in inflammatory bowel disease, autoimmune hepatitis, and other chronic inflammatory conditions --- these are NOT considered evidence of vasculitis
  \item The 2017 International Consensus on ANCA Testing recommends that all positive ANCA by IIF should be followed by antigen-specific testing for PR3 and MPO, and that ELISA should be used as the primary screening method where IIF is not available \cite{bossuyt2017revised}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Anti-PR3 and anti-MPO ELISA (antigen-specific testing):} Must be performed when ANCA is positive by IIF to determine the target antigen. The combination of IIF pattern and ELISA specificity guides the specific ANCA-associated vasculitis diagnosis
  \item \textbf{Kidney biopsy:} The gold standard for diagnosis of ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis. Light microscopy shows focal segmental necrotizing glomerulonephritis with crescents. Immunofluorescence is characteristically pauci-immune (scant or absent immunoglobulin deposition), distinguishing it from immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis (SLE, IgA nephropathy) and anti-GBM disease
  \item \textbf{Lung biopsy (video-assisted thoracoscopic or open):} For patients with pulmonary nodules, cavities, or alveolar hemorrhage when kidney biopsy is not diagnostic. Histology shows necrotizing granulomatous inflammation and vasculitis
  \item \textbf{Urinalysis and urine microscopy:} Detection of dysmorphic red blood cells, red blood cell casts, and proteinuria indicative of glomerular bleeding and glomerulonephritis
  \item \textbf{Serum creatinine and eGFR:} Assessment of renal function at diagnosis and for monitoring
  \item \textbf{Spot urine protein-to-creatinine ratio or 24-hour urine protein:} Quantification of proteinuria
  \item \textbf{Anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) antibody:} To exclude Goodpasture syndrome in patients presenting with pulmonary-renal syndrome
  \item \textbf{Chest CT (high-resolution):} To evaluate for pulmonary nodules, cavities, infiltrates, and alveolar hemorrhage
  \item \textbf{Nasal/sinus biopsy:} For patients with GPA presenting with chronic sinusitis, nasal crusting, or saddle-nose deformity
  \item \textbf{Complete blood count with differential and eosinophil count:} Eosinophilia (>1,500/\textmu L or >10\%) is characteristic of EGPA
\end{itemize}
