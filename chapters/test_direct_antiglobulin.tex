\chapter{Direct Antiglobulin Test (Coombs Test)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
The direct antiglobulin test detects immunoglobulin or complement attached to a patient's red blood cells in the circulation. It is a key laboratory test for immune-mediated hemolysis.

\section*{Alternative Names}
DAT; Direct Coombs Test; Direct Antiglobulin Reaction.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
It is used in suspected autoimmune hemolytic anemia, hemolytic transfusion reactions, hemolytic disease of the fetus or newborn, and selected drug-related hemolysis. It helps distinguish immune red-cell destruction from nonimmune causes, but it must be interpreted with evidence of hemolysis.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
Patient red cells are washed and exposed to antihuman globulin reagent. Agglutination indicates that IgG, complement, or both are present on the red-cell surface. Monospecific reagents can distinguish IgG from complement coating.

\section*{Preparation, Risks, and Limitations}
No fasting is required and risks are limited to venipuncture. A positive result does not prove that hemolysis is occurring, and a negative result does not exclude every immune hemolytic process. Recent transfusion, medicines, and technical factors can affect interpretation.

\section*{Understanding Results}
A positive DAT with anemia, elevated reticulocytes, indirect bilirubin, and LDH and reduced haptoglobin supports immune hemolysis. The clinical pattern and transfusion history determine whether the likely cause is autoimmune, transfusion-related, neonatal, or drug-associated.

\section*{References}
British Society for Haematology guidance on autoimmune hemolytic anemia and pretransfusion testing.
