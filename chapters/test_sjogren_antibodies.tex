\chapter{Sjögren Antibodies (Anti-SSA/Ro and Anti-SSB/La)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
Anti-SSA/Ro and anti-SSB/La are autoantibodies associated with Sjögren disease and several other systemic autoimmune disorders. They are measured in blood as part of an autoimmune evaluation; a positive result alone does not establish a diagnosis.

\section*{Alternative Names}
\begin{itemize}
  \item SSA Antibody
  \item Ro Antibody
  \item SSB Antibody
  \item La Antibody
  \item Sjögren Syndrome Antibody Panel
\end{itemize}
\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
Testing may be appropriate for persistent dry eyes or mouth, salivary-gland enlargement, systemic features suggestive of Sjögren disease, lupus or another connective-tissue disease, or selected pregnancy assessments because maternal anti-SSA antibodies can affect the fetus.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
A blood sample is tested by an immunoassay, commonly with an extractable nuclear antigen panel. Results should be interpreted alongside ANA testing, symptoms, examination, and other laboratory findings.

\section*{Preparation, Risks, and Limitations}
No fasting is required and risk is limited to venipuncture. Anti-SSA can occur in lupus and other diseases, and some patients with clinically diagnosed Sjögren disease are antibody-negative. Anti-SSB without anti-SSA is less specific. A positive result should not be used to diagnose autoimmune disease in an asymptomatic person.

\section*{Understanding Results}
Positive anti-SSA or anti-SSB supports, but does not prove, an autoimmune connective-tissue disease. A negative result does not exclude Sjögren disease. In pregnancy, a positive anti-SSA result may lead to specialist monitoring because of the small risk of fetal conduction abnormalities.

\section*{References}
American College of Rheumatology and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification guidance for Sjögren disease.
