\chapter{C-Reactive Protein (CRP)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
C-reactive protein (CRP) is an acute phase protein synthesized by the liver in response to tissue injury, infection, and inflammation. CRP derives its name from its ability to precipitate the C-polysaccharide of Streptococcus pneumoniae in the presence of calcium. It is a pentameric protein consisting of five identical 23-kDa subunits arranged in a cyclic disk, belonging to the pentraxin protein family (calcium-dependent ligand-binding plasma proteins) \cite{pepys2003creactive}. CRP is a pattern recognition receptor that binds to phosphocholine on microbial surfaces and damaged host cell membranes, activating the classical complement pathway via C1q binding and promoting opsonization and phagocytosis of pathogens and cell debris. CRP is one of the most sensitive acute phase reactants --- levels rise within 4--6 hours of an inflammatory stimulus, peak at 24--72 hours (reaching levels 100--1,000 times baseline), and fall rapidly (half-life approximately 19 hours) when the stimulus resolves, making it an excellent marker for monitoring the course of inflammation and infection. The high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) assay, with a lower detection limit of approximately 0.01--0.1 mg/L, is used for cardiovascular risk stratification, while the standard CRP assay (detection limit 3--5 mg/L) is used for assessment of infection and inflammation.

\section*{Alternative Names}
CRP; C-Reactive Protein; High-Sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP); Cardiac CRP; Quantitative CRP; Pentraxin-1

\section*{Principle}
CRP is measured by particle-enhanced immunoturbidimetry or immunonephelometry on automated clinical chemistry analyzers. Latex particles coated with anti-human CRP monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies are incubated with patient serum. CRP in the sample binds to the antibody-coated particles, causing agglutination. The resulting increase in turbidity (light scattering) is measured photometrically at a wavelength of approximately 570--600 nm and is directly proportional to the CRP concentration. Results are calibrated against the international reference standard CRM 470 (now ERM-DA470k/IFCC). Standard CRP assays have a functional sensitivity (lower limit of quantitation) of approximately 3--5 mg/L, which is adequate for detecting clinically significant elevations in inflammation and infection. High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) assays use enhanced detection methods (more sensitive latex particles, kinetic nephelometry) to achieve a functional sensitivity of 0.01--0.1 mg/L, enabling measurement of low-grade inflammation relevant to cardiovascular risk prediction. Point-of-care CRP assays using lateral flow immunochromatography are available for rapid testing in outpatient settings.

\section*{History}
CRP was discovered in 1930 by William Tillett and Thomas Francis at Rockefeller University, who described a non-type-specific precipitin reaction between sera of patients with pneumococcal pneumonia and the C-polysaccharide (cell wall polysaccharide) of Streptococcus pneumoniae \cite{tillett1930serological}. The protein was named ``C-reactive protein'' by Maclyn McCarty in 1947 after successive purification. The acute phase response and the diagnostic value of CRP in inflammatory and infectious diseases were extensively studied in the 1950s--1970s. The development of highly sensitive immunoassays (ELISA, nephelometry) in the 1980s--1990s revolutionized CRP measurement, and the landmark Physician's Health Study (Ridker et al., 1997) and subsequent trials demonstrated that elevated hs-CRP predicts future myocardial infarction and stroke independent of traditional risk factors. The 2003 AHA/CDC Scientific Statement endorsed hs-CRP for cardiovascular risk assessment in intermediate-risk patients. The JUPITER trial (2008) demonstrated that rosuvastatin significantly reduced cardiovascular events in individuals with elevated hs-CRP ($\geq$2.0 mg/L) and normal LDL cholesterol, providing evidence for targeting inflammation in cardiovascular prevention \cite{ridker2008rosuvastatin}.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Detection and monitoring of systemic inflammation and infection in patients with suspected sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis, pyelonephritis, appendicitis, or other bacterial infections
  \item Differentiation of bacterial from viral infection --- CRP is markedly elevated in bacterial infections (>100 mg/L in severe cases) but only mildly elevated or normal in uncomplicated viral infections (typically <40 mg/L). Used alongside procalcitonin for antibiotic stewardship
  \item Monitoring disease activity and treatment response in chronic inflammatory conditions: rheumatoid arthritis (CRP is a component of the DAS28 disease activity score), Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis, giant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, and systemic lupus erythematosus (though CRP may be discordantly low in active SLE despite active inflammation)
  \item Assessment of post-operative complications --- CRP normally peaks at 48--72 hours after major surgery and falls thereafter. A secondary rise or failure to fall suggests a post-operative infection (wound infection, abscess, anastomotic leak)
  \item Cardiovascular risk stratification using hs-CRP: Low risk <1.0 mg/L, Average risk 1.0--3.0 mg/L, High risk >3.0 mg/L for future cardiovascular events (the 2003 AHA/CDC Scientific Statement recommends hs-CRP measurement for individuals at intermediate 10-year Framingham risk, 10--20\%)
  \item Evaluation of suspected neonatal sepsis --- CRP is a sensitive marker for neonatal bacterial infection when measured serially; two normal CRP levels 24 hours apart have a high negative predictive value
  \item Monitoring of antibiotic therapy response --- CRP levels that fall by >50\% in 24--48 hours suggest appropriate antibiotic selection and favorable clinical response
  \item Detection of occult infections in immunocompromised patients (chemotherapy-induced neutropenia, transplant recipients) in whom clinical signs of infection may be masked
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
CRP is a secondary non-specific test. It serves as a sensitive marker of inflammation and infection but does not identify the cause. CRP is used for screening, monitoring disease activity, and guiding clinical decision-making in conjunction with history, physical examination, and disease-specific testing.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
For most patients, a health care professional will take a blood sample from a vein in your arm using a small needle. After the needle is inserted, a small amount of blood will be collected into a test tube or vial. You may feel a little sting when the needle goes in or out. This usually takes less than five minutes.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
No special preparation is required. Fasting is not necessary for standard CRP measurement. For hs-CRP measurement for cardiovascular risk assessment, the 2003 AHA/CDC guidelines recommend that patients should be clinically stable and free of acute illness or inflammation at the time of measurement. If hs-CRP is >10 mg/L, acute infection or inflammation should be excluded and the test repeated in 2--3 weeks. hs-CRP measurement should be performed twice (2 weeks apart) and the average used for cardiovascular risk stratification in asymptomatic individuals.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
No absolute contraindications. Specimen should be collected in a serum separator tube (SST, gold-top) or plasma (lithium heparin, green-top tube). Grossly hemolyzed or lipemic specimens may interfere with measurement. CRP is elevated by numerous conditions and does not identify the cause of inflammation. Conditions that elevate CRP: acute and chronic infections (bacterial, fungal, parasitic, some viral), autoimmune and inflammatory conditions (rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, vasculitis, gout), tissue necrosis (myocardial infarction, pancreatitis, trauma, burns, major surgery), malignancy (particularly lymphoma, renal cell carcinoma, and advanced solid tumors), obesity (adipose tissue produces IL-6 which stimulates hepatic CRP production), smoking, diabetes mellitus, estrogen therapy and oral contraceptives (increase hepatic CRP synthesis), pregnancy (physiologic elevation), and intense exercise. Medications that lower CRP: statins, corticosteroids, NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), anti-TNF biologics, aspirin, and fish oil (omega-3 fatty acids). CRP is synthesized by the liver, and hepatic synthetic failure (severe cirrhosis, end-stage liver disease) can produce falsely low CRP despite significant inflammation.

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk. Slight pain or bruising at the venipuncture site. Rare: hematoma, infection, vasovagal syncope.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results are typically available within 1 to 2 hours (or within 15--20 minutes for point-of-care assays). Markedly elevated CRP (>100 mg/L) in a febrile patient strongly suggests a bacterial infection and warrants investigation for its source (blood cultures, urinalysis and culture, chest radiograph, lumbar puncture if indicated) and initiation of empiric antibiotic therapy. Elevated CRP in a patient with known inflammatory arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease is compared to previous values to assess treatment response. An elevated hs-CRP (>2.0--3.0 mg/L) for cardiovascular risk assessment in an intermediate-risk patient may influence the intensity of statin therapy and lifestyle modification recommendations.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item CRP <3 mg/L (standard assay) or <1.0 mg/L (hs-CRP assay): Indicates absence of significant systemic inflammation. A normal CRP level argues against a serious bacterial infection but does not exclude localized infection or mild viral illness
  \item CRP normalization after an elevated value indicates resolution of the inflammatory or infectious process and is a favorable prognostic sign
  \item A normal CRP (<5 mg/L) in a febrile patient with convincing clinical evidence of infection does not exclude a bacterial process --- CRP has a sensitivity of 70--90\% for bacterial infection and may be falsely normal in early infection (<6 hours), localized infection, or infections in immunosuppressed patients
  \item Low CRP in active SLE: Patients with active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) may have discordantly low or normal CRP despite significant active disease (including serositis, arthritis, and active lupus nephritis). A markedly elevated CRP in SLE raises concern for superimposed bacterial infection rather than lupus flare
  \item False-negative CRP in hepatic failure: End-stage liver disease with severely impaired synthetic function can produce a blunted CRP response despite significant bacterial infection, reducing the reliability of CRP as an infection marker in this population
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mild elevation (CRP 3--10 mg/L): Low-grade inflammation associated with obesity, smoking, diabetes mellitus, chronic periodontitis, chronic stress, sleep deprivation, and sedentary lifestyle. Cardiovascular risk assessment using hs-CRP: 1.0--3.0 mg/L indicates average cardiovascular risk; >3.0 mg/L indicates high risk
  \item Moderate elevation (CRP 10--50 mg/L): Mild to moderate systemic inflammation seen in viral infections (influenza, adenovirus, CMV, EBV), moderate bacterial infections (urinary tract infection, bronchitis), autoimmune disease flares (rheumatoid arthritis), and postoperative state
  \item Marked elevation (CRP 50--100 mg/L): Active systemic inflammation suggestive of more significant infection (pneumonia, pyelonephritis, diverticulitis), autoimmune disease exacerbation (severe rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease flare, systemic vasculitis), and tissue injury (pancreatitis, trauma)
  \item Very high elevation (CRP >100 mg/L): Severe bacterial infection (septicemia, bacterial meningitis, severe pneumonia, intra-abdominal sepsis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis). CRP >200 mg/L is strongly suggestive of a significant bacterial infection and warrants aggressive investigation for the source
  \item CRP >500 mg/L: Massive acute inflammatory response seen in severe bacterial sepsis, extensive burns, and severe trauma
  \item CRP trajectory: The rate of change of CRP provides important clinical information. CRP that rises rapidly over 24--48 hours suggests active, uncontrolled infection. CRP that falls by >50\% over 24--48 hours with antibiotic therapy suggests appropriate treatment and a favorable prognosis. A secondary rise after an initial fall suggests a new complication (superinfection, abscess formation, development of an anastomotic leak)
  \item hs-CRP >3.0 mg/L for cardiovascular risk: Associated with a 2--3-fold increase in the risk of future myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death, independent of LDL cholesterol and other traditional risk factors
  \item hs-CRP >10.0 mg/L: If not associated with acute infection or inflammation, is associated with markedly elevated cardiovascular risk. However, levels >10 mg/L should prompt a search for an acute inflammatory process, and hs-CRP should be remeasured in 2--3 weeks once the acute process has resolved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Standard CRP assay:} <3--5 mg/L (laboratory-dependent cutoff)
  \item \textbf{High-Sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) for cardiovascular risk:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Low cardiovascular risk: <1.0 mg/L
    \item Average cardiovascular risk: 1.0--3.0 mg/L
    \item High cardiovascular risk: >3.0 mg/L
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Post-operative:} CRP normally peaks at 50--150 mg/L at 48--72 hours after major abdominal or cardiothoracic surgery, then declines with a half-life of approximately 19 hours
  \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} CRP may be mildly elevated (approximately 2--3 times non-pregnant levels)
  \item \textbf{Newborns:} CRP <5 mg/L in healthy term infants. CRP >10 mg/L raises concern for neonatal sepsis (with serial measurements improving diagnostic accuracy: sensitivity ~90\%, specificity ~85\%, and NPV >95\% if two normal values 24 hours apart)
  \item \textbf{Obesity (BMI >30 kg/m$^2$):} Higher baseline CRP (1--10 mg/L) due to adipocyte-derived IL-6 stimulating hepatic CRP production
  \item The hs-CRP reference ranges for cardiovascular risk should not be used in the setting of acute or chronic inflammatory conditions, as the hs-CRP in these situations reflects inflammation rather than cardiovascular risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Procalcitonin (PCT):} Used alongside CRP for the differentiation of bacterial infection (PCT elevated) from viral infection or non-infectious inflammation (PCT normal or low). PCT rises more rapidly (within 2--4 hours) than CRP (4--6 hours) during bacterial infection and is increasingly used to guide antibiotic therapy decisions (antibiotic stewardship programs)
  \item \textbf{Complete blood count (CBC) with differential:} Total white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and band forms (left shift) provide complementary information to CRP for evaluation of infection and inflammation. Neutrophilia supports bacterial infection
  \item \textbf{Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR):} A non-specific measure of inflammation. ESR rises more slowly (24--48 hours) and falls more slowly (over days to weeks) than CRP. ESR is more affected by anemia, pregnancy, age, and hypergammaglobulinemia than CRP. ESR and CRP are complementary --- measuring both detects more cases of inflammation than either alone
  \item \textbf{Blood cultures (two sets, aerobic and anaerobic):} If bacterial sepsis is suspected. Should be drawn before initiating antibiotic therapy whenever possible
  \item \textbf{Site-specific cultures:} Urine culture (suspected UTI/pyelonephritis); sputum culture (suspected pneumonia); cerebrospinal fluid analysis and culture (suspected meningitis); wound and tissue cultures (abscess, cellulitis); peritoneal fluid culture (suspected spontaneous bacterial peritonitis)
  \item \textbf{Imaging:} Chest radiograph (suspected pneumonia); abdominal/pelvic CT with contrast (suspected intra-abdominal infection or abscess); echocardiography (suspected infective endocarditis); extremity imaging and MRI (suspected osteomyelitis)
  \item \textbf{Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibody:} If rheumatoid arthritis is suspected based on symmetric polyarthritis and elevated CRP
  \item \textbf{ANA and anti-dsDNA antibody:} If systemic lupus erythematosus is suspected (discordantly low CRP in SLE should raise suspicion for flare rather than infection)
  \item \textbf{Fasting lipid profile:} If hs-CRP is elevated for cardiovascular risk assessment. Elevated hs-CRP combined with an elevated LDL cholesterol identifies patients who benefit most from aggressive statin therapy (per JUPITER trial criteria and ACC/AHA guidelines)
\end{itemize}
