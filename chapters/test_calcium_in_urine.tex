\chapter{Calcium (Urine)}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
The urine calcium test measures the amount of calcium excreted in the urine over 24 hours or in a random spot urine sample. Urinary calcium excretion reflects the balance between dietary calcium intake, intestinal calcium absorption, bone resorption, and renal tubular calcium reabsorption. In healthy adults consuming a normal calcium diet (approximately 1,000 mg/day), approximately 150--300 mg of calcium is excreted in urine per 24 hours (50--300 mg/day in most reference ranges). Approximately 60--70\% of dietary calcium is not absorbed and is excreted in feces. Of the calcium that enters the bloodstream, only about 1--2\% is excreted in urine, with the remainder either deposited in bone or utilized intracellularly. In the kidney, approximately 65\% of filtered calcium is reabsorbed passively in the proximal tubule (paracellularly along with sodium and water), 20\% in the thick ascending limb of Henle (paracellularly driven by the positive luminal potential from Na-K-2Cl cotransporter activity---blocked by loop diuretics), and 10--15\% is actively reabsorbed in the distal convoluted tubule under the regulation of parathyroid hormone (PTH) and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D. Only about 1--2\% of filtered calcium is excreted in the final urine. Urine calcium measurement is essential for the evaluation of kidney stones, calcium metabolism disorders, and parathyroid disease.

\section*{Alternative Names}
24-Hour Urine Calcium; Urinary Calcium; Urine Ca; 24h Urine Calcium Excretion; Spot Urine Calcium/Creatinine Ratio; Fractional Excretion of Calcium

\section*{Principle}
Urine calcium is measured by the same spectrophotometric methods used for serum calcium: o-cresolphthalein complexone (CPC) or arsenazo III colorimetric methods. The urine sample is acidified (to pH <2) with hydrochloric acid to dissolve any calcium precipitates (calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate) before analysis. The addition of acid ensures that all calcium in the sample is dissolved and measured. For 24-hour collections, the total calcium excretion is calculated by multiplying the calcium concentration (mg/dL) by the total urine volume (dL) over 24 hours. For spot urine samples, the calcium-to-creatinine ratio is calculated and correlates reasonably well with 24-hour excretion: Ca/Cr <0.14 mg/mg is normal; Ca/Cr >0.20 mg/mg indicates hypercalciuria. The test is performed on automated chemistry analyzers. Urine calcium measurement does not differ substantially from serum calcium measurement; the key difference is sample preparation (acidification of urine).

\section*{History}
The role of urine calcium in kidney stone formation has been recognized since the early 20th century. The introduction of 24-hour urine collections for metabolic stone evaluation was pioneered by Fredric Coe and Charles Pak in the 1970s. Pak established the classification of hypercalciuria subtypes (absorptive, renal leak, resorptive) in the 1970s \cite{pak1979}. The calcium-creatinine ratio as a simpler alternative to 24-hour collections was validated in the 1980s-1990s.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaluation of recurrent calcium nephrolithiasis (kidney stones): hypercalciuria is present in 30--60\% of calcium stone formers and is the most common metabolic abnormality identified
  \item Diagnosis and monitoring of primary hyperparathyroidism: elevated PTH increases both bone resorption (releasing calcium) and renal calcium reabsorption---net effect is usually hypercalciuria (65--80\% of patients)
  \item Differentiation of familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH) from primary hyperparathyroidism: FHH is characterized by lifelong mild hypercalcemia with inappropriately low urine calcium excretion (<100 mg/24h, calcium-to-creatinine clearance ratio <0.01)
  \item Evaluation of suspected vitamin D toxicity: excess vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption, leading to hypercalciuria and hypercalcemia
  \item Assessment of renal tubular acidosis (RTA): distal RTA (Type I) is associated with hypercalciuria due to bone buffering of chronic acidosis and decreased renal citrate excretion (hypocitraturia), promoting calcium phosphate stone formation
  \item Monitoring of dietary calcium restriction or thiazide diuretic therapy in calcium stone formers: thiazides decrease urinary calcium excretion by increasing proximal and distal calcium reabsorption
  \item Evaluation of osteoporosis: a 24-hour urine calcium helps differentiate causes of bone loss and guides treatment. Low urine calcium (<50--100 mg/24h) suggests malabsorption or vitamin D deficiency; high urine calcium (>300--400 mg/24h) suggests hypercalciuria as a contributor to bone loss
  \item Diagnosis of idiopathic hypercalciuria: the most common cause of calcium stones, characterized by increased urine calcium with normal serum calcium, normal PTH, and no identifiable secondary cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
Urine calcium is a secondary test used for specific indications, primarily the evaluation of nephrolithiasis, calcium metabolism disorders, and parathyroid disease. It is not a routine screening test.

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
\textbf{24-hour urine collection:} The patient discards the first morning void on Day 1 and collects all urine for the next 24 hours, including the first morning void on Day 2. The urine container should be kept refrigerated or on ice during collection. The total 24-hour volume is measured, and an aliquot is sent for calcium measurement. \textbf{Random spot urine:} A single urine sample (preferably second morning void) is collected. Both calcium and creatinine are measured, and the calcium-to-creatinine ratio is calculated. The spot Ca/Cr ratio is a reasonable screening test (particularly in children) but less precise than a 24-hour collection. The sample must be acidified (HCl to pH <2) before analysis to prevent precipitation of calcium salts.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
The patient should follow their usual diet (approximately 800--1,200 mg calcium/day) for accurate assessment. A very low-calcium or very high-calcium diet in the days preceding the test will alter results. The following medications affect urine calcium and should be noted: (1) Increase urine calcium: loop diuretics (furosemide---inhibit calcium reabsorption in thick ascending limb), high-dose vitamin D/calcitriol, calcium supplements, glucocorticoids (increase bone resorption), high-sodium diet (increases calcium excretion), excessive protein intake (increases acid load and calcium excretion), caffeine. (2) Decrease urine calcium: thiazide diuretics (increase distal calcium reabsorption---most potent drug effect, reducing Ca by 30--40\%), amiloride, bisphosphonates, estrogen, calcitonin. The patient should maintain adequate hydration during the 24-hour collection. All urine must be collected---missing even one void invalidates the test. The container should be refrigerated and protected from light. Acid preservative (HCl) in the collection container is standard.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item No absolute contraindications. Important considerations:
  \item (1) Incomplete collection is the most common error---if the total 24-hour urine creatinine falls outside the expected range (15--20 mg/kg/day for females, 20--25 mg/kg/day for males), the collection was likely incomplete and results are unreliable.
  \item (2) Non-acidified urine allows calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate to precipitate, resulting in falsely low calcium measurement. Laboratories typically check that the urine pH is <3 before analysis.
  \item (3) Severe hematuria from active stone passage can falsely elevate urine calcium.
  \item (4) Recent IV calcium or phosphate administration alters results.
  \item (5) Children: urine calcium:creatinine ratio >0.21 mg/mg (>0.6 mmol/mmol) is abnormal.
  \item (6) Pregnancy: urine calcium increases due to increased GFR and increased calcitriol-mediated intestinal absorption.
  \item (7) Immobilization: prolonged bed rest increases bone resorption and urine calcium; a 24-hour urine calcium is abnormal until the patient has been ambulating for several days.
  \item (8) Diet: sodium intake beyond 5,000--6,000 mg/day significantly increases calciuria; protein intake over 1.5 g/kg/day also increases it. Assess dietary factors.
\end{itemize}
\section*{Risks}
No direct risk to the patient from urine collection. The main challenge is patient compliance with complete 24-hour collection, which can be inconvenient. No blood draw is required.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results are typically available within 1--2 days. Urine calcium interpretation: (1) Hypercalciuria: >300 mg/24h (males), >250 mg/24h (females), or >4 mg/kg/24h. In patients with recurrent calcium oxalate stones, the goal is to reduce urine calcium to <250 mg/24h (males) or <200 mg/24h (females). (2) If hypercalciuria is present with normal serum calcium (normocalcemic hypercalciuria): consider idiopathic hypercalciuria, dietary factors, loop diuretics, RTA, or sarcoidosis. (3) If hypercalciuria with hypercalcemia: primary hyperparathyroidism, malignancy, vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali syndrome. Check PTH. (4) If hypocalciuria (<50--100 mg/24h) with hypercalcemia: strongly suggests FHH (mutated calcium-sensing receptor). (5) If hypocalciuria with normal calcium: thiazide use, dietary calcium deficiency, malabsorption, vitamin D deficiency. Treatment of hypercalciuria: thiazide diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide 25--50 mg/day, chlorthalidone 25 mg/day), dietary sodium restriction (<2,300 mg/day), moderate calcium intake (800--1,200 mg/day---paradoxically, low-calcium diets increase stone risk by increasing oxalate absorption), limit animal protein \cite{pearle2014}.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypocalciuria (urine Ca <50--100 mg/24h):} Very low urine calcium is an important diagnostic clue.
  \item \textbf{Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH):} Inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor (CASR) in parathyroid and renal tubule. Labs: lifelong mild hypercalcemia (10.5--12 mg/dL), normal or mildly elevated PTH, very low 24h urine calcium (<100 mg/24h), Ca/Cr clearance ratio <0.01. Benign condition---does not require parathyroidectomy (unlike primary hyperparathyroidism, which also has elevated PTH and calcium but with normal or high urine calcium). Critical to differentiate from primary hyperparathyroidism to avoid unnecessary surgery.
  \item \textbf{Thiazide diuretic therapy:} Therapeutic effect of thiazides in hypercalciuric stone formers. Decrease urine Ca by 30--40\%.
  \item \textbf{Dietary calcium deficiency or malabsorption:} Low intestinal calcium absorption limits urinary excretion. Celiac disease, Crohn's disease, short bowel syndrome, vitamin D deficiency, chronic pancreatitis.
  \item \textbf{Hypoparathyroidism:} Low PTH decreases bone resorption and renal calcium mobilization, reducing filtered calcium load.
  \item \textbf{Advanced CKD:} Severely reduced GFR limits calcium filtration, decreasing urine calcium regardless of serum level.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hypercalciuria (>300 mg/24h males, >250 mg/24h females, or >4 mg/kg/24h):} Most common metabolic abnormality in calcium stone formers \cite{worcester2010}.
  \item \textbf{Idiopathic hypercalciuria (most common cause):} Normocalcemic hypercalciuria without identifiable secondary cause. Three subtypes described by Pak: (a) Absorptive hypercalciuria: increased intestinal calcium absorption (possibly from increased calcitriol sensitivity or vitamin D receptor upregulation). Fasting urine calcium normal (bone resorption not elevated); post-load calcium excretion is high. (b) Renal leak hypercalciuria: primary renal tubular defect in calcium reabsorption causing mild hypocalcemia, which stimulates PTH, increasing calcitriol and bone resorption. Fasting urine calcium elevated with elevated PTH. (c) Resorptive hypercalciuria: primary increase in bone resorption. Rare, seen in primary hyperparathyroidism.
  \item \textbf{Primary hyperparathyroidism:} Elevated PTH increases bone resorption and calcitriol-mediated intestinal calcium absorption, increasing filtered calcium load. Urine calcium is typically >300 mg/24h but may be normal or even low in some patients. Differentiated from FHH by Ca/Cr clearance ratio >0.01 and typically higher 24h urine calcium.
  \item \textbf{Vitamin D toxicity/over-supplementation:} Excess vitamin D increases intestinal calcium absorption, causing hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria.
  \item \textbf{Granulomatous disease:} Sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, berylliosis, histoplasmosis---macrophages in granulomas express 1-alpha-hydroxylase and produce calcitriol autonomously, independent of PTH regulation. Hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, suppressed PTH, elevated 1,25(OH)\textsubscript{2}D (or normal but inappropriately high given hypercalcemia).
  \item \textbf{Renal tubular acidosis (distal RTA, Type I):} Chronic metabolic acidosis causes bone dissolution (buffer) and hypocitraturia. Elevated urine calcium with urine pH >5.5 despite systemic acidosis, and low urine citrate (<320 mg/24h).
  \item \textbf{Loop diuretics:} Furosemide blocks calcium reabsorption in thick ascending limb, promoting hypercalciuria.
  \item \textbf{Immobilization:} Prolonged bed rest or paralysis increases bone resorption, causing hypercalciuria and hypercalcemia.
  \item \textbf{Malignancy:} Hypercalcemia with hypercalciuria (increased filtered load from bone metastases, PTHrP production, or calcitriol production by lymphoma).
  \item \textbf{High dietary sodium, protein, or caffeine intake:} All increase renal calcium excretion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{24-hour urine calcium (adults, normal calcium diet):} Males: 100--300 mg/24h; Females: 100--250 mg/24h
  \item \textbf{24-hour urine calcium (low-calcium diet, 400 mg/day):} <200 mg/24h
  \item \textbf{Spot urine calcium/creatinine ratio (adults):} <0.14 mg/mg (<0.40 mmol/mmol) is normal; >0.20 mg/mg indicates hypercalciuria
  \item \textbf{Spot urine calcium/creatinine ratio (children):} <0.21 mg/mg (<0.60 mmol/mmol); age-dependent
  \item \textbf{Fractional excretion of calcium (FECa):} 1--2\% normally. >2\% in hypercalciuria; <1\% in FHH
  \item \textbf{Calcium/creatinine clearance ratio:} >0.01 in primary hyperparathyroidism; <0.01 in FHH
  \item \textbf{Conversion:} 1 mg = 0.025 mmol; 1 mmol = 40 mg
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serum calcium (total and ionized), albumin, PTH:} Essential for differential diagnosis of hypercalciuria. Elevated Ca + elevated PTH $\rightarrow$ primary hyperparathyroidism. Normal Ca + elevated PTH $\rightarrow$ could be renal leak hypercalciuria or early hyperparathyroidism.
  \item \textbf{Serum phosphorus, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D:} For vitamin D-mediated hypercalciuria.
  \item \textbf{Urine citrate, oxalate, and uric acid (complete 24h stone risk profile):} Hypocitraturia (<320 mg/24h) is present in 20--60\% of stone formers. Hyperoxaluria (>45 mg/24h) increases calcium oxalate stone risk. Urine pH <5.5 promotes uric acid stones. This panel also includes urine volume, creatinine, sodium, calcium, phosphate, magnesium.
  \item \textbf{Serum creatinine and eGFR:} Assess renal function.
  \item \textbf{Kidney imaging (non-contrast CT or ultrasound):} Detect stones, nephrocalcinosis.
  \item \textbf{Calcium loading test (Pak test):} Formal assessment of absorptive vs. renal vs. resorptive hypercalciuria subtypes using fasting and post-calcium-load urine calcium measurements. Rarely performed in modern practice.
  \item \textbf{CASR gene testing:} If FHH is suspected.
  \item \textbf{ACE level:} If sarcoidosis is suspected.
\end{itemize}
