\chapter{C-Peptide}

\section*{What Is This Test?}
Connecting peptide (C-peptide) is a 31-amino-acid polypeptide that connects the A and B chains of proinsulin. During insulin biosynthesis in pancreatic beta cells, preproinsulin is cleaved to proinsulin, which is then cleaved by prohormone convertases (PC1/3 and PC2) and carboxypeptidase E in secretory granules to yield equimolar amounts of insulin and C-peptide. Both are co-secreted into the portal circulation upon beta cell stimulation. C-peptide has a longer plasma half-life (30--35 minutes) than insulin (4--6 minutes) and undergoes minimal hepatic extraction (unlike insulin, which undergoes 50--60\% first-pass hepatic clearance). C-peptide has no known biological function on its own, though recent research suggests possible effects on microvascular function and renal blood flow. Because C-peptide is secreted in equimolar amounts to insulin but is not contained in commercial insulin preparations and is not significantly cleared by the liver, its measurement provides a more accurate reflection of endogenous beta cell insulin secretion than insulin measurement itself. The C-peptide test is essential for: (1) distinguishing endogenous hyperinsulinism (insulinoma, sulfonylurea use) from exogenous insulin administration (factitious or therapeutic), (2) assessing residual beta cell function in diabetes mellitus, (3) classifying diabetes type, and (4) evaluating hypoglycemia.

\section*{Alternative Names}
Connecting Peptide; Serum C-Peptide; Plasma C-Peptide; Proinsulin C-Peptide; Insulin C-Peptide; C-Peptide by Immunoassay; Fasting C-Peptide; Stimulated C-Peptide (Glucagon or Mixed Meal Stimulation)

\section*{Principle}
C-peptide is measured using a two-site sandwich immunometric (chemiluminescent) immunoassay on automated analyzers. Two monoclonal antibodies against different epitopes on the C-peptide molecule are used (C-peptide is immunologically distinct from insulin and proinsulin, allowing specific measurement with minimal cross-reactivity). Common platforms: Roche Cobas ECLIA, Abbott Architect CMIA, Siemens Atellica/ADVIA Centaur CLIA. The functional sensitivity is typically <0.01--0.05 ng/mL. The assay is calibrated against the WHO International Standard for human C-peptide (IRP 84/510). C-peptide is relatively stable: collected into serum separator tubes or EDTA tubes, stable at room temperature for 24 hours, at 2--8 degrees C for 7 days, and at -20 degrees C for 6 months. Importantly, C-peptide does not cross-react with insulin, proinsulin (usually <5\%), or any insulin analogues. Hemolyzed specimens do not degrade C-peptide (unlike insulin, which is rapidly degraded by insulin-degrading enzyme from erythrocytes), making C-peptide more reliable in suboptimally handled specimens. In urine, 5--10\% of secreted C-peptide is excreted unchanged, and 24-hour urinary C-peptide provides an integrated measure of 24-hour insulin secretion.

\section*{History}
C-peptide was discovered by Donald Steiner and colleagues at the University of Chicago in 1967 during studies of insulin biosynthesis, who demonstrated that insulin is derived from a single-chain precursor (proinsulin) linked by C-peptide \cite{steiner1967insulin}. The first radioimmunoassay for C-peptide was developed by Melani and colleagues in 1970. C-peptide measurement became clinically widespread in the 1980s for evaluating hypoglycemia and distinguishing insulinoma from factitious hypoglycemia. Its role in distinguishing type 1 from type 2 diabetes was established in the 1980s--1990s. The 2009 Endocrine Society clinical practice guideline on adult hypoglycemic disorders (Cryer et al.) codified the use of C-peptide in hypoglycemia evaluation \cite{cryer2009evaluation}. The clinical utility of C-peptide in diabetes classification is now integrated into ADA and WHO guidelines, with stimulated or fasting C-peptide measurement recommended when diabetes type is uncertain.

\section*{Why This Test Is Ordered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Distinguishing endogenous hyperinsulinism (insulinoma, sulfonylurea-induced) from exogenous insulin administration (factitious or therapeutic) in hypoglycemia --- elevated C-peptide (>0.6 ng/mL) with hypoglycemia and elevated insulin indicates endogenous source; suppressed C-peptide (<0.6 ng/mL) with elevated insulin indicates exogenous insulin administration
  \item Diagnosis of insulinoma during the supervised 72-hour fast --- elevated C-peptide (>0.6 ng/mL) at the time of hypoglycemia (glucose <55 mg/dL) with elevated insulin confirms endogenous hyperinsulinism
  \item Assessment of residual beta cell function in diabetes: absent/very low C-peptide (<0.2--0.5 ng/mL fasting) confirms type 1 diabetes or advanced beta cell failure; preserved C-peptide (>1--2 ng/mL fasting) with elevated glucose indicates type 2 diabetes with insulin resistance
  \item Classifying diabetes type when clinical features are ambiguous (e.g., obese patient with presumed type 1 diabetes, thin patient with presumed type 2 diabetes, or monogenic diabetes)
  \item Monitoring beta cell function in islet cell transplantation or pancreas transplantation --- rising C-peptide indicates graft function
  \item Detection of surreptitious sulfonylurea or meglitinide use --- elevated C-peptide with positive sulfonylurea screen differentiates from insulinoma (requires toxicology screen)
  \item Mixed meal stimulation test assessing beta cell reserve in diabetes --- C-peptide measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120 minutes after a standardized liquid meal (Boost/Sustacal)
  \item Investigation of post-bariatric surgery hypoglycemia (dumping syndrome)
  \item Evaluation of neonatal hypoglycemia with suspected congenital hyperinsulinism
\end{itemize}

\section*{Primary vs Secondary Test}
C-peptide is a primary test in the hypoglycemia evaluation (alongside glucose and insulin) and a secondary (adjunctive) test in diabetes classification. In hypoglycemia, C-peptide is essential because insulin alone cannot distinguish endogenous from exogenous insulin. In diabetes, C-peptide is used when clinical classification is uncertain and has implications for therapy (e.g., distinguishing type 1 requiring insulin from type 2 manageable with oral agents).

\section*{How This Test Is Performed}
A healthcare professional draws a blood sample from a vein into a serum separator tube (gold-top) or EDTA tube (~3--5 mL). For hypoglycemia evaluation, the sample must be drawn at the time of spontaneous hypoglycemia (glucose <55 mg/dL) and sent STAT. For diabetes classification, a fasting sample is drawn, and in some cases, stimulated C-peptide is measured after a mixed meal (Boost/Sustacal 6 mL/kg, max 360 mL) or 1 mg glucagon IV/IM. For the 72-hour fast, samples are drawn at the time of critical hypoglycemia. C-peptide is stable and does not require special pre-analytical handling beyond standard blood processing.

\section*{What Preparation Is Needed}
Fasting for 8--12 hours is required for basal C-peptide assessment. For stimulated C-peptide testing, the mixed meal or glucagon stimulation is performed per protocol. High-dose biotin (>5 mg/day) must be held 48 hours before testing. In diabetes, C-peptide measurement should be performed when glucose is >70 mg/dL to avoid the confounding effect of hypoglycemia-induced beta cell suppression. For hypoglycemia evaluation, the sample must be drawn at the time of spontaneous symptoms. Glucagon stimulation test: 1 mg glucagon IV or IM, C-peptide and glucose measured at 0 and 6 minutes. A C-peptide <0.6 ng/mL at 6 minutes indicates absent insulin reserve (type 1 diabetes). Mixed meal stimulation: C-peptide measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 180 minutes after standardized liquid meal.

\section*{Contraindications and Precautions}
C-peptide is only interpretable with a simultaneously drawn glucose. C-peptide is appropriately suppressed during fasting hypoglycemia (<0.6 ng/mL with glucose <55 mg/dL) in normal individuals. An elevated C-peptide at this time is inappropriate and indicates endogenous hyperinsulinism. In renal failure (CKD stages 4--5), C-peptide clearance is reduced and C-peptide levels are elevated (2--5 fold above normal) independent of insulin secretion. This can confound the diagnosis of both insulinoma (false-positive elevation) and assessment of beta cell function. For patients with CKD, the interpretation of C-peptide is complex and requires nephrology-endocrinology collaboration. Proinsulin cross-reactivity in C-peptide assays is typically <5\% with modern assays, but proinsulin can be disproportionately elevated in insulinoma, so this small cross-reactivity is generally not clinically significant. In type 2 diabetes with severe insulin resistance, C-peptide is appropriately elevated (fasting >2--4 ng/mL), reflecting compensatory hyperinsulinemia. In obesity, C-peptide is elevated due to insulin resistance-driven beta cell compensation. C-peptide must be interpreted in context of glucose: a normal C-peptide with hyperglycemia is actually inappropriately low (beta cell failure). C-peptide is preferred over insulin for assessing beta cell function because it avoids hepatic extraction variability and insulin assay cross-reactivity issues. Insulin:C-peptide molar ratio: normally <1.0 (equimolar secretion, but insulin has shorter half-life). Ratio >1.0 indicates exogenous insulin (insulin present without corresponding C-peptide).

\section*{Risks}
Minimal risk. Slight pain, bruising, or hematoma at venipuncture site. For mixed meal or glucagon stimulation: mild nausea from liquid meal or glucagon (common), very rarely vomiting. Hypoglycemia may occur after stimulation testing in insulinoma patients; supervised monitoring is required.

\section*{What Happens After the Test}
Results available within 1--4 hours. Interpretation in hypoglycemia (glucose <55 mg/dL at the time of blood draw): C-peptide >0.6 ng/mL with elevated insulin confirms endogenous hyperinsulinism (insulinoma or sulfonylurea-induced). A negative sulfonylurea/meglitinide screen is required to exclude factitious drug use before proceeding to insulinoma localization. C-peptide <0.6 ng/mL with elevated insulin confirms exogenous insulin administration. For diabetes classification: fasting C-peptide <0.2--0.5 ng/mL with hyperglycemia indicates severe insulin deficiency (type 1 diabetes, pancreatogenic diabetes, or advanced type 2). Fasting C-peptide >1--2 ng/mL with hyperglycemia indicates insulin resistance (type 2 diabetes). Stimulated C-peptide <0.6 ng/mL after mixed meal or glucagon indicates absent beta cell reserve necessitating insulin therapy.

\section*{Understanding Results}

\subsection*{Below Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Type 1 diabetes mellitus: autoimmune destruction of beta cells causes progressive C-peptide decline. At diagnosis, C-peptide may be detectable (0.2--1.0 ng/mL, "honeymoon phase") but declines to undetectable (<0.1 ng/mL) within 2--5 years. Fasting C-peptide <0.2--0.5 ng/mL and stimulated <0.6 ng/mL confirms absolute insulin deficiency.
  \item Advanced type 2 diabetes: beta cell failure after years of insulin resistance results in low C-peptide despite hyperglycemia. Fasting may be normal or low, stimulated response is blunted. This is the indication for transitioning from oral agents to insulin therapy.
  \item Latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA): slowly progressive autoimmune beta cell destruction in adults (often misdiagnosed as type 2 diabetes). C-peptide is initially preserved (1--2 ng/mL) but declines over 3--6 years. Anti-GAD65 antibodies confirm.
  \item Pancreatectomy or chronic pancreatitis: loss of beta cell mass causes insulin deficiency. C-peptide is low. Patients require insulin therapy.
  \item Exogenous insulin administration: C-peptide is SUPPRESSED (<0.6 ng/mL during hypoglycemia) because exogenous insulin does not contain C-peptide and beta cells are suppressed by hypoglycemia and excess insulin. The insulin:C-peptide molar ratio is >1.0. This is the key finding in factitious hypoglycemia from surreptitious insulin injection.
  \item Prolonged fasting/starvation with hypoglycemia: appropriate suppression of C-peptide to <0.6 ng/mL as the normal response to low glucose.
  \item Post-islet cell or pancreas transplant failure: declining then absent C-peptide indicates transplant rejection or graft failure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Above Normal}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Insulinoma: elevated C-peptide (>0.6 ng/mL) with hypoglycemia (glucose <55 mg/dL) and elevated insulin. C-peptide is elevated because insulinoma cells process proinsulin and secrete C-peptide along with excess insulin. Proinsulin is also elevated (proinsulin >20\% of total insulin immunoreactivity). The insulin:C-peptide molar ratio is <1.0 (both elevated from endogenous secretion). Negative sulfonylurea screen is required.
  \item Sulfonylurea or meglitinide-induced hypoglycemia: identical biochemical pattern to insulinoma (elevated insulin, C-peptide, proinsulin with hypoglycemia) because these drugs stimulate endogenous insulin secretion from beta cells. Positive drug screen on toxicology differentiates this from insulinoma. Surreptitious sulfonylurea use is the most common cause of factitious hypoglycemia (more common than surreptitious insulin because oral ingestion is easier).
  \item Type 2 diabetes with insulin resistance: compensatory hyperinsulinemia causes elevated fasting C-peptide (often >2--4 ng/mL, up to 10 ng/mL) with elevated glucose. This reflects robust beta cell compensation for insulin resistance. As beta cells fail over years, C-peptide declines.
  \item Obesity (without diabetes): C-peptide is elevated (fasting >2--3 ng/mL) due to insulin resistance-driven compensatory hyperinsulinemia. HOMA-IR is elevated.
  \item PCOS: mild-to-moderate C-peptide elevation reflecting insulin resistance, which is a central pathophysiologic feature.
  \item Chronic kidney disease (CKD stages 4--5): C-peptide is renally cleared (unlike insulin, which is primarily hepatically cleared). Decreased GFR leads to C-peptide accumulation. Fasting C-peptide >4--8 ng/mL may be seen in patients on dialysis without endogenous hyperinsulinism. Interpretation must account for renal function.
  \item Post-islet cell or pancreas transplant function: elevated C-peptide indicates functioning graft.
  \item Congenital hyperinsulinism (nesidioblastosis): neonatal or infantile hypoglycemia with inappropriately elevated C-peptide.
  \item Dumping syndrome / post-gastric bypass hypoglycemia: rapid nutrient delivery to small intestine causes excessive GLP-1 and insulin secretion. C-peptide is inappropriately elevated at the time of postprandial hypoglycemia 1--4 hours after eating.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Normal Results}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fasting C-peptide (euglycemic, insulin-sensitive): 0.8--3.0 ng/mL (0.26--1.0 nmol/L)
  \item Fasting C-peptide in type 2 diabetes with insulin resistance: 2--6+ ng/mL
  \item Fasting C-peptide in type 1 diabetes (established): <0.2--0.5 ng/mL (often <0.1 ng/mL)
  \item Stimulated C-peptide (mixed meal or glucagon): >1.5--3.0 ng/mL (normal beta cell reserve); <0.6 ng/mL (absent reserve, type 1 diabetes)
  \item C-peptide during hypoglycemia (glucose <55 mg/dL): <0.6 ng/mL (appropriately suppressed); >0.6 ng/mL (inappropriate, endogenous hyperinsulinism)
  \item Insulin:C-peptide molar ratio: <1.0 (endogenous secretion); >1.0 (exogenous insulin administration)
  \item 24-hour urinary C-peptide: 15--60 mcg/24 hours (integrated measure of insulin secretion)
  \item Note: Reference ranges are assay- and laboratory-specific. C-peptide must be interpreted with simultaneous glucose. In CKD, C-peptide is elevated independent of insulin secretion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Follow-up Tests}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Simultaneous plasma glucose and insulin: C-peptide is always interpreted together with glucose and insulin. The integrated pattern is necessary for diagnosis.
  \item Proinsulin: Elevated in insulinoma. Proinsulin-to-insulin ratio >30\% suggests insulinoma.
  \item Beta-hydroxybutyrate: Suppressed in insulin-mediated hypoglycemia, elevated in non-insulin-mediated.
  \item Sulfonylurea/meglitinide toxicology screen: Essential in all cases of hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia to exclude surreptitious drug use before proceeding to insulinoma imaging.
  \item Anti-insulin antibodies: For insulin autoimmune syndrome (Hirata disease).
  \item Anti-GAD65, anti-IA2, anti-ZnT8 antibodies: Confirm type 1 diabetes or LADA when C-peptide is low.
  \item Abdominal CT/MRI and endoscopic ultrasound: Localizing insulinoma.
  \item Selective arterial calcium stimulation with hepatic venous insulin sampling: Regionalizing insulinoma when imaging is negative.
  \item Mixed meal stimulation test: Evaluating beta cell reserve and hypoglycemia patterns.
\end{itemize}
